# The Philosopher-King Test

**Reading the AI industry through its terms and its money**

Son-U Michael Paik · CEO, GRC Solutions Korea

1 November 2026

> The self-appointed philosopher-king is usually neither.
>
> LinkedIn, 31 October 2026

**Starting point.** On 31 October 2026 (Korea Standard Time, KST) I published a short LinkedIn post that opens with the line above ([post](https://lnkd.in/p/guVakvKW)). It used Plato, three animals and a pharaoh to make one point: people who present themselves as philosopher-kings are best read by following their money. This piece turns that point into a method for understanding the AI industry and cutting through its hype. It names no living person. Its examples are institutions' published documents, cited by title and date.

**For:** anyone who has to make sense of what the AI industry says about itself. That includes a board asked to approve a purchase, a buyer reading a vendor's deck, an auditor scoping an engagement, an adviser asked whether artificial general intelligence (AGI) is near and a journalist on deadline. The doctrine is [DOCTRINE.md](https://github.com/rolldabones/rolldabones/blob/main/DOCTRINE.md) and the method is the [Slow AI Kitchen](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen). The piece adds a reading method and one table to take into a meeting. It adds no doctrine, step, gate, tier or form.

**Durability.** The documents in Sections 4 and 5 are dated evidence, and some will be superseded within months. That is part of the argument. The test is meant to outlast them. Section 9 records where it is most likely to be wrong.

**A declared interest.** This piece was prepared with Claude, a model made by Anthropic, and Section 5 links my series on Anthropic's securities filing. Readers should apply the test in Section 7 to this piece as well.

## 1. The applicant

Plato set out the idea in the *Republic*. There would be no rest from troubles for cities, he has Socrates say, until philosophers rule as kings or those now called kings genuinely philosophize (473c-d). The line is quoted far more often than the condition Plato attached to it two books later. The city whose prospective rulers are least eager to rule, he says, is the one best and most peaceably governed, and the city with the opposite kind of rulers is governed worst (520d). His philosopher-rulers have to be compelled to take office; they would rather be doing philosophy.

That condition is the first test, and it disqualifies the applicant. Anyone who campaigns for the role of philosopher-king has failed Plato's own entry requirement before saying anything else. The argument of this piece is therefore not against Plato. It is against the applicants, and it uses Plato to make it.

Plato also ran the pilot study. By the account of the Seventh Letter, he went to Syracuse in 367 BC at the invitation of the young tyrant Dionysius II, whose uncle Dion was Plato's friend and student. Dion was soon exiled, and Plato was lodged in the citadel, from which, he says, no ship's captain would have dared take him against the ruler's will (329e-330a). Dionysius sat through one exposition of the doctrine, gave out that he already knew the important parts and later wrote a treatise of his own on it (341a-b). On a third visit, in 361 BC, Plato again had trouble leaving and got home only after his friend Archytas and the Tarentines sent a thirty-oared ship for him (350a-b). Dion later took Syracuse by force and was murdered in 354 BC by Callippus, a member of Plato's Academy. The Seventh Letter's authenticity is disputed, which is why the account is attributed to it rather than stated as fact.

The AI industry has an unadvertised version of the post, and it is never short of applicants. They give speeches about the destiny of the species, the end of work and the shape of the next civilization, and the firms they speak for sell, fund or build the thing being described. Eagerness to explain the future is not evidence about the future. On Plato's terms it is a reason for caution.

## 2. The pen

The "alpha wolf" came out of studies of captive wolves, beginning with Rudolf Schenkel's of 1947, and L. David Mech's 1970 book *The Wolf* carried the term into general use. Mech then spent thirteen summers watching wild wolves on Ellesmere Island and in 1999 published the correction. Most research on pack dynamics, he wrote, had been done on "non-natural assortments of captive wolves"; in nature "the typical wolf pack is a family," with the parents guiding it. The "alphas" are the parents. The dominance contests of the captive studies came from confinement. Unrelated adults who could neither leave nor hunt produced posturing, a pecking order and nothing to show for it.

The AI industry's bubble is a pen of the same kind. A small number of firms, investors and researchers talk mostly to one another and to audiences who cannot check what they hear: a keynote crowd, a conference panel, a podcast host who does not push back. Display in a pen tells you about the pen. It tells you nothing about how the animal would hunt.

The practical point for a governance reader is to ask where a claim has been put that it could fail. Three venues qualify. The first is an evaluation the speaker did not design and cannot rerun until it passes. The second is a contract with remedies. The third is a filing that carries liability: under section 11 of the Securities Act of 1933, a buyer of securities can sue the signers, directors and underwriters when a registration statement contained an untrue statement of a material fact when it became effective (15 U.S.C. § 77k(a)). A claim made only on a stage has not yet left the pen. The wording a claim must survive in a registration statement is the wording worth reading.

## 3. The loan

At Burgers' Zoo in Arnhem, Frans de Waal watched adult male chimpanzees trade the top position, and wrote it up as *Chimpanzee Politics* (1982). Power moved by coalition, never by vision. Luit had just regained the top in 1980 when Nikkie and Yeroen, whose alliance had broken down, closed ranks again and attacked him one night in the sleeping quarters. Luit died of his wounds. The chimpanzees understood what applicants forget: the top is held on loan from the others, and the loan can be called in.

The periodical cicada makes the second point. It spends thirteen or seventeen years underground, emerges in synchronized broods that can reach a million or more insects an acre, makes a great deal of noise for about four weeks, mates and dies. The empty shells stay on the trees.

Ozymandias makes the third. Ramesses II ruled Egypt for sixty-six years and built as if the invoice would never arrive. Diodorus Siculus recorded the inscription on one of his colossal statues: if anyone would know how great he was and where he lay, let him surpass one of his works (*Bibliotheca historica* 1.47). His lasting review came from Percy Bysshe Shelley, aged 25, who never saw Egypt, in a sonnet printed in *The Examiner* on 11 January 1818: two vast and trunkless legs, a shattered visage and, on the pedestal, the boast with nothing beside it.

The AI industry runs on all three patterns. A place at the top of a capability ranking is held the way Luit held his. Announcements emerge by the brood and are loud for a few weeks. The models they announced are retired on published deprecation schedules, and the press releases stay on the trees. The governance consequence is narrow and useful: **the date of a claim is part of the claim.** A claim recorded without its date and the version it refers to cannot be checked later, and in this industry later comes quickly.

## 4. Different turtles

The post allowed itself one relapse into philosophy, and it was Zeno. In the paradoxes Aristotle reports (*Physics* VI.9), a runner who must first cover half the distance, then half of what remains, never arrives. Achilles never overtakes the tortoise because it has always moved on by the time he reaches where it was. Much of AI discourse moves the same way. The argument gets halfway to a definition of a term, the term is redefined and the race restarts from the new position.

"Turtles all the way down" is the old joke about infinite regress. The post's addition was "a different turtle each time." A regress is bad enough. A regress whose terms are swapped as it goes is worse, because nothing said later can be checked against what was said before.

Hype claims about AI come in four kinds: capability (what a system can do), timeline (when something will arrive), safety (what will not be deployed, or what is being guarded against) and market (how large the opportunity is). Each kind leans on terms that can move. The table sets out six, each from published documents, side by side and dated. It records what each text says and nothing about why it changed.

| Term | One published use | A later or competing use | What moved |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AGI** | OpenAI Charter (April 2018): "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work." | OpenAI, "The next chapter of the Microsoft–OpenAI partnership" (28 October 2025): once OpenAI declares AGI, "that declaration will now be verified by an independent expert panel"; the revenue share runs until the panel verifies AGI, and Microsoft's research intellectual property rights until then or through 2030, whichever is first. OpenAI, "The next phase of the Microsoft OpenAI partnership" (27 April 2026): revenue share payments to Microsoft "continue through 2030, independent of OpenAI's technology progress"; the announcement does not use the term. | From a description of capability, to a contractual trigger decided by a panel, to a term the latest partnership terms do not need. |
| **Safety** (a tracked risk) | OpenAI, *Preparedness Framework (Beta)* (18 December 2023): Persuasion is one of four Tracked Risk Categories, "focused on risks related to convincing people to change their beliefs." | OpenAI, *Preparedness Framework, Version 2* (15 April 2025): "Persuasion category risks do not fit the criteria for inclusion"; they are handled through other policies. The same version provides that, on conditions it states, OpenAI may adjust its required safeguards if another developer releases a high-risk system without comparable ones. | The set of risks the framework commits to measure, and the conditions on which its safeguards hold. |
| **Safety** (a scaling commitment) | Anthropic, "Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy" (19 September 2023): the system means Anthropic would have to "temporarily pause training of more powerful models" if scaling outpaced its safety procedures; the commitments are "our current best guess," and changes need board approval after consulting the Long Term Benefit Trust. | Anthropic, "Announcing our updated Responsible Scaling Policy" (15 October 2024): the update "introduces a more flexible and nuanced approach," built on Capability Thresholds that trigger Required Safeguards, after a first year that showed "we needed to incorporate more flexibility into our policies." | From commitments labeled provisional to a more flexible structure, announced by the developer with its reasons. The first document also set the procedure for changing it, which gives the reader something to check. |
| **Open source** | Meta, Llama 2 announcement (18 July 2023): "our open source large language model." The Llama 2 Community License of the same date requires licensees above 700 million monthly active users to request a license and bars using the materials to improve any other large language model. | Open Source Initiative, *Open Source AI Definition 1.0* (28 October 2024): the freedoms to use, study, modify and share the system "for any purpose," with data information, code and parameters. | A vendor's word against an independent steward's definition. The reader compares the license terms with "for any purpose." |
| **Agent** | Anthropic, "Building effective agents" (19 December 2024): agents are "systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage," distinct from workflows that follow "predefined code paths." | OpenAI, "A practical guide to building agents" (2025; the page is undated): "Agents are systems that independently accomplish tasks on your behalf." | Two vendors, one word: one definition turns on who controls the process, the other on the outcome delivered. A product can be an agent under one and a workflow under the other. |
| **AI system** | European Commission, proposal for the AI Act, COM(2021) 206 (21 April 2021), Article 3(1): software developed with the techniques listed in Annex I (machine learning, logic- and knowledge-based and statistical approaches). | Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (13 June 2024; Official Journal of 12 July 2024), Article 3(1): "a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy" that "infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs." | The legal scope of the whole Act, moved by the legislature in public, with every version dated and kept. |

The last row is the control case. Terms do change, and should, as a field learns. The AI Act's definition moved a long way between proposal and Regulation, but every version was published, dated and attributable, so anyone can say which definition governed on which day. The fault the table exposes is not change. It is change that leaves no trail, or a word used commercially as though no one else had defined it.

Two terms often moved in this way, reasoning and alignment, are not in the table. This edition did not establish a clean pair of dated primary texts for either, and a row resting on inference would defeat the purpose of the table. Intelligence appears only inside the rows for AGI and AI system.

The point for governance, risk management and compliance is plain. **A control cannot be written against a term that moves.** A policy that permits "agents" with human oversight, a contract that warrants a system is "open source" or a board paper that plans for "AGI" by a date has no fixed object until the term is pinned. The fix costs one paragraph: quote the definition relied on, name the document, its version and its date, and treat any later change to that definition as a change event that triggers review. In a contract, the paragraph can read like this, adapted with counsel:

> "Agent" has the meaning given in [document title], [version], published [date], quoted in full in Schedule [X]. A later change to that definition by [Vendor] does not change this Agreement. [Vendor] will notify [Client] in writing within [30] days of any such change, and the parties will review the affected controls within [30] days of the notice.

The same paragraph works in a policy or a risk register, with the owner of the review named in place of the parties. This is the companion to [*The Map and the Pair*](the-map-and-the-pair.md), which gives the account's vocabulary for reading claims that a model has discovered something. That piece reads the claim. This one reads the institution making it.

## 5. Follow the money

Money does not take part in the Zeno race. It arrives or it does not, and it leaves a record. The post put the test as three questions: who pays him, whom he pays and what the philosophy is for. Applied to an industry rather than a person, the first two become questions about an institution, and each has public records that answer it, in part.

**Who pays the institution.** Securities filings answer for public companies and companies going public: the registration statement and each amendment to it, then annual and current reports, all searchable on the EDGAR system of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Exempt offerings by private companies leave a thinner record, a Form D notice filed on EDGAR within 15 days of the first sale. Funding and grant announcements, government contract awards and the counterparties named in partnership announcements fill in the rest.

**Whom the institution pays.** Lobbying registers record spending to influence legislators and officials: in the United States, reports under the Lobbying Disclosure Act on LDA.gov; in the European Union, the Transparency Register run by the European Commission. Declared board and advisory positions, published compensation and equity structures in proxy statements and the published terms of partnerships complete the picture.

The method has three rules, and they are part of it. It reads public records only. It reads institutions and their published claims, never private individuals. It compiles nothing about anyone. And it asks of a public claim one thing: what public money sits behind it. Where the record is silent, the answer is Unknown, which is a finding, not a failure.

The AGI row in Section 4 shows the method at work, because its documents are themselves money records. In October 2025 the verification of AGI governed a revenue share and the life of intellectual property rights. In April 2026 the revenue share ran "independent of OpenAI's technology progress," and the term dropped out of the announcement. The piece draws no inference about why. It does not need to. Set side by side, the two documents show the reader what the word was carrying and when it stopped carrying it.

This account practices the method on one filing. My series on the risk factors in Anthropic's first public S-1 (filed 26 October 2026) reads them one at a time, in the order filed, for the mitigations each implies; it begins with [risk factor 1 of 88](https://lnkd.in/p/gTaZg7YC). The series is linked as an example of the practice, not for its conclusions.

## 6. What the philosophy is for

The third question is the operative one. A stated philosophy in the mouth of an applicant is usually an instrument, and the first two questions locate what it is used for. A philosophy of inevitability fits a request for capital and an argument against regulation. A philosophy of danger can support spending on safety and also an argument that only a few firms should be allowed to build. A philosophy of openness can support adoption and also the free use of other people's work. None of these patterns proves anything about a given institution. Each is a hypothesis the money records can test, and the reader should expect to find some of them false.

The test takes three steps. First, state the philosophy in one sentence, in the institution's own published words where possible. Second, list the decisions it asks its audience to make: invest, buy, adopt, refrain from regulating, trust a commitment. Third, for each decision, name the record in which it would show up if it were made, and read that record. A philosophy whose decisions all land in the institution's own revenue, funding or regulatory position is doing a job. One whose decisions cost the institution something, and are seen to be kept, is evidence of a different kind.

Two footnotes show how easily the material moves while it is being quoted. The stock Latin of Occam's Razor, *entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*, is "nowhere to be found in his texts," as the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* puts it. Ockham wrote *pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate*, plurality is not to be posited without necessity. The modern wording, *non sunt multiplicanda entia sine necessitate*, appears first in a commentary by the Irish Franciscan John Punch (Ponce) of Cork in Luke Wadding's 1639 edition of Duns Scotus, where Punch calls it a common axiom of the Scholastics without naming one. Ockham himself fled the papal court at Avignon in May 1328, with the head of his order, after a dispute with Pope John XXII over Franciscan poverty, so the man whose razor is quoted had followed the money in his own way. And "follow the money" is not from Watergate. It was written into the screenplay of the 1976 film *All the President's Men* and appears nowhere in Woodward and Bernstein's 1974 book. Even its authorship has moved: the screenplay is credited to William Goldman, and Goldman and Woodward have each credited the other with the line.

Both attributions moved while being quoted, which is the Zeno problem in miniature. The discipline is the same as for terms: check the source of a famous line as you would the definition of a word, and record what you found.

## 7. The test on one page

The table turns the method into the questions a governance reader can take into a meeting. Each row is one kind of claim. "Ask" is the question to put to whoever made it. "Read" is the record that answers it. "What it shows" is what the answer tells you, which is never whether the claim is true.

| Claim | Ask | Read | What it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capability ("our model reasons," "discovered") | Under which definition, of which date? Where has it been tested in a way that could have failed? | The model or system card and its evaluation method; [*The Map and the Pair*](the-map-and-the-pair.md) for discovery claims | Whether the claim has left the pen |
| Timeline ("AGI within a few years") | What does the term mean in the institution's own contracts and filings? | Charters, partnership announcements, securities filings | Whether the term carries money, and since when |
| Safety ("we will not deploy without...") | Which version of the framework, what changed since the last, and was the change made by the procedure the framework itself sets? | The framework's version history and its amendment clause | Whether the commitment moved, when and by whose decision |
| Market ("a trillion-dollar opportunity") | Who pays the institution, and whom does it pay? | Registration statements and amendments, Form D, lobbying registers, partnership terms | What the projection is for |
| Openness ("open source") | By whose definition? | The license text against the Open Source AI Definition | Whether the word fits the terms |
| Agency ("autonomous agent") | Agent under which definition, and who answers for what it does? | Product documentation and the contract's allocation of responsibility | Where liability sits when the agent is wrong |

## 8. Two doctrines, stated once

The philosopher-king answers to no one. That is the condition the account's doctrine exists to prevent. As stated in DOCTRINE.md: **Final Liability** rests with the Human: every material outcome attaches to a named human owner with decision rights, oversight and the power to intervene. The last row of the Section 7 table is that doctrine as a question to a vendor.

The second connection is **Informed Intent**: no AI system acts without prior authorization stating its purpose, authority, boundaries and exit, given by a human who meets the five authorizer conditions. An applicant's philosophy is usually a substitute for stating intent in that checkable form. A purpose can be audited. A vision cannot.

Both are quoted in their short forms from [DOCTRINE.md, section 8](https://github.com/rolldabones/rolldabones/blob/main/DOCTRINE.md#8-short-forms), the account's single normative statement of the doctrines. This piece adds nothing to them.

## 9. Where this argument is weakest

**Money explains motive, not truth.** A heavily funded claim can be correct, and an unfunded one wrong. The test tells a reader what a claim is for and how much weight to give the speaker's confidence. It does not tell the reader whether the claim is true. That needs the evidence the claim rests on, and *The Map and the Pair* is the account's method for reading it.

**Terms evolve for good reasons.** The AI Act row shows a definition improving in public. A reader who treats every change of definition as bad faith will be wrong often and unfair to institutions doing exactly what the table recommends.

**The records are uneven.** The money method works best on public companies in jurisdictions with lobbying registers. Private companies disclose little, and much of the industry is private. Many answers will be Unknown, and the method has to say so rather than fill the gap.

**The table is a sample, not a measurement.** The six rows were chosen because clean pairs of dated documents exist for them. They show that terms move; they do not show how often, or that movement is typical of any institution named. The four claim types are the author's working classification and have not been tested against a body of claims.

**The test can be misused.** Following the money can turn into a reflex that dismisses every claim by pointing at its funding. That is the ad hominem fallacy applied to institutions. The table asks what a claim is for; it does not license ignoring it.

**The author has interests.** They are declared at the top of this piece. The reader is invited to apply the test to it.

## 10. The one thing not giving a speech

Everything else in this piece performs: the tyrant who publishes after one lesson, the captive wolves, the cicada, the coalition in the night quarters and the pedestal in the sand. Terms perform too, carried from one argument to the next and changed on the way. Money does none of that. It sits in the record, dated. It is the one thing in the room not giving a speech. The Tao Te Ching, which the post preferred to Plato, ranks rulers in chapter 17 and puts first the age in which, in Legge's translation, "(the people) did not know that there were (their rulers)." By that standard the most reliable voice in any discussion of the AI industry is the one that never speaks.

Follow the money.

## Questions for discussion

1. Pick one term your organization uses in an AI policy or contract ("agent," "autonomous," "open source," "safe"). Whose definition is it and of which date? Where is that written down?
2. Which claim about AI has most influenced a decision in your organization in the past year? Where, if anywhere, was it put so that it could fail?
3. For the vendor you rely on most, what do its public money records show, and what are they silent on?
4. When a vendor's safety framework or definition changes, who in your organization would notice, and what would they do?
5. Section 6 offers three philosophies as instruments. Find a case where the money records falsify one of them.
6. Where does Final Liability sit for the agentic system your organization is closest to deploying, and does the contract say the same thing?
