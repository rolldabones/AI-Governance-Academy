# The Core and the Capability

**From strategic outsourcing to the governed AI enterprise: a discussion piece**

Son-U Michael Paik · CEO, GRC Solutions Korea

15 November 2026

> Quinn asked us to organize around intellect. Now AI asks whether intellect itself can be decomposed, delegated and governed.
>
> LinkedIn, May 2026

**Starting point.** In 1992, in Japan, I heard James Brian Quinn describe what he called the intelligent enterprise. He was teaching at the International University of Japan, in a program built with Dartmouth's Amos Tuck School, and I was in his class. His argument was that a firm competes by managing intellect and not by owning assets, and his test was ruthless: be best in the world at an activity or move it outside. The corporation, he said, becomes less a fortress than a spider web. I wrote about that class in a [LinkedIn post](https://lnkd.in/p/gHnsay72) in May 2026, and the line above is from it.

This piece is the long form of that post, and it has a companion. [*Claude's Plugin Families and The Slow AI Kitchen*](claude-plugin-families-and-the-slow-ai-kitchen.md) lists what one AI vendor now sells: ready-made packages for sales, finance, legal, human resources, engineering and a dozen other kinds of work. Read that list slowly and it is an organization chart on a shelf. The companion asks how people stay in charge of one workflow. This piece asks the question that comes next: when the departments can be bought as packages, how much of the organization does the business still need, and what must it keep in order to remain a business at all?

**For:** owners, executives and board members, before they select automation candidates or set workforce targets. No knowledge of AI is assumed. A reader with ten minutes reads the brief and Sections 6, 9 and 12.

**What this piece is and is not.** It is a design hypothesis with its tests attached. It does not claim that experiments on individual tasks have shown that radically smaller firms work. It adds no doctrine, step, gate, tier or form to this account; it uses one working term, assured delegation, which Section 6 defines and ties to an existing doctrine. It states no law except three dated examples in Section 9, given to show the kind of duty that applies and not what applies to any reader.

## In brief

1. AI makes it possible to keep a capability without keeping the staffing model that used to deliver it. Those are different things, and confusing them is the first mistake.
2. Quinn showed that a firm need not perform every activity it depends on. He also insisted that it keep what makes it distinctive. AI carries his question inside the core: what must remain human-executed for a competence to survive?
3. The best field evidence so far supports testing new configurations of people and AI. It does not support a staffing plan. In that evidence, the most exceptional results came from teams with AI, not from individuals with AI.
4. The limit on a smaller core is not how much work AI can do. It is how much delegated work the organization can remain competent to direct.
5. The default test is whether the same people, better equipped, create more value. A smaller organization may follow from the evidence. It should not lead it.

## 1. The organization chart on the shelf

The companion map counts seventeen job-function plugins from one vendor: sales, customer support, marketing, finance, legal, human resources, engineering, design, operations and more. Beside them sit packages for whole industries, from banking to manufacturing. Each is a set of written instructions and system connections that makes an AI model work like a specialist in that job.

Nobody designed that shelf as a picture of a company. It is one all the same. A generation ago a firm that wanted a finance function hired one. Then it could outsource one. Now it can install a first draft of one before lunch.

That changes the question an executive is asked. The familiar question is how to make existing employees more productive, and the plugin answers it at the level of the task. The harder question sits one level up: if substantial parts of a department's work can be delegated to software that the firm directs, what is the department for, how many people does it need and which of them can the firm not do without?

This piece takes that question seriously and tries to keep it honest. The companion map makes a claim about people: for every plugin, name the humans first. This piece makes the same claim about the organization. A firm that redesigns itself around what the software can do, before deciding what its people must still be able to do, has started at the wrong end.

## 2. What remains when execution can be delegated

An organization chart records a settlement between work, technology and authority at a moment in time. It should not be mistaken for a permanent description of what a business needs.

Consider a firm that produces specialist analysis for Clients. It must understand the Client's problem, gather the evidence, reach a defensible conclusion and deliver something useful. Until recently that took several people working in sequence: research, drafting, checking, approval.

If AI can do substantial parts of that work to an acceptable standard, the firm has a choice. It can make the existing sequence faster. Or it can reconsider the sequence, the allocation of responsibility and the number of people needed to sustain it. The second choice is the consequential one.

A capability can remain central to a business even when people no longer perform every activity that supports it. What matters is whether the enterprise can still direct the work, tell acceptable performance from failure, improve its methods and keep its commitments.

That distinction separates **retaining a capability** from **retaining its historical staffing model**.

It also sets a hard test. A firm that can commission impressive outputs but cannot evaluate or recover the underlying work has not kept a capability. It has rented a service and lost command of its own business.

## 3. Quinn: the boundary of the firm

In 1994 Quinn and Frederick Hilmer published *Strategic Outsourcing*. Its opening sets out two moves to be made together. The firm concentrates its own resources on a small set of core competencies where it can be preeminent and give customers something unique. It then outsources other activities, "including many traditionally considered integral to any company", where it has neither a strategic need nor a special capability. Their example was Nike, then the world's largest supplier of athletic shoes, which outsourced all of its shoe production and concentrated on research and development at one end and on marketing, distribution and sales at the other.

The idea of core competence was not Quinn's alone. C.K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel had named it in 1990, and their point is worth holding beside his: a competence lives in what an organization has learned to do together, not in any one product or department. That matters later in this piece. Something learned collectively can be lost collectively.

By 2000, in *Outsourcing Innovation*, Quinn had carried the argument well past factories and support functions. The sources of new knowledge outside any firm, he argued, had grown too numerous for one company to match alone, and the strategic management of outsourcing was "perhaps the most powerful tool in management". Knowledge work and innovation were inside his argument a quarter of a century ago. It would be wrong to summarize him as keeping the thinkers and outsourcing the rest.

What he did not have to consider is the question AI now puts: **what must remain human-executed for the firm to retain a strategically important competence?** That is an extension proposed here. It is not a claim about what Quinn predicted.

Three things are different this time.

**The supplier is not a firm.** Quinn's suppliers were companies with their own people, incentives and expertise, often knowing more about their specialty than the buyer did. A plugin is a set of instructions that the vendor wrote for a typical customer, running on a model the vendor operates. It knows nothing about your business until your people write it in. The companion map makes this point at length: the plugin is a generic starting point, and the organization's own way of working has to be put into it by the people who do the work.

**The choices have multiplied.** An activity can now be performed by the firm's own people, by an outside professional firm, by conventional software or by an AI workflow that the firm directs, and one capability can combine all four. Where the technology runs is a separate matter again. A model the firm hosts can do delegated work, and a model hosted elsewhere can support work the firm directs closely. Who hosts, who performs and who holds authority over the result are three questions, not one.

**The boundary now runs through the core.** Quinn drew a line around the firm's distinctive competence and outsourced what lay beyond it. AI draws a second line inside the competence itself, between directing the work and executing it. That inner line is the subject of the rest of this piece.

In my post I put the destination this way: the company does not disappear, and its reason to exist shifts, from a hierarchy for moving information to a legal and fiduciary shell around intelligence, capital, data and accountability. The virtual corporation is becoming the agentic corporation. The question is whether governance evolves with it, or whether we build faster spider webs and call it strategy.

## 4. Drucker: what the capability is for

Quinn explains how a firm may be arranged. Peter Drucker explains what the arrangement is for. The purpose of a business, in his formulation, is to create a customer, and so it has two basic functions: marketing and innovation.

Read as a principle of organizational design, that is a discipline. More productive capacity is not the goal. Serving someone well enough that the enterprise deserves to continue is the goal.

Marketing, in this reading, means understanding whom the business serves, what they value and why they would choose it. Innovation means finding better ways to create that value. Neither is a department name, and neither is a protected human occupation: AI can help with research, synthesis, experiment and design in both. What stays with people is choosing the direction of the enterprise and accepting its commitments.

Three responsibilities follow, and a redesigned firm must still be able to carry all three.

| Responsibility | The question it must answer |
|---|---|
| Entrepreneurial direction | Whom will we serve, what will we offer and why should it matter? |
| Operating design | Which combination of people, systems, information and outside services can deliver it? |
| Governance and assurance | What may that combination do, what evidence supports relying on it and who can intervene? |

In a small firm one person may hold several of these. That does not make them the same job.

AI governance, risk management and compliance belongs inside this management system and not beside it. It cannot supply a missing customer proposition, substitute for commercial judgment or make an uneconomic business viable by documenting it more carefully.

## 5. What the evidence supports

The best field evidence on this question is a preregistered experiment at Procter & Gamble, published in the journal *Organization Science* in June 2026 after circulating as a working paper from 2025. The published analysis covers the 791 professionals who completed the exercise. Each worked on a real product-innovation challenge from their own business unit, in a one-day workshop, either alone or in a two-person team that paired a commercial specialist with a technical one, and either with or without an AI tool.

Three of its findings bear on organizational design.

**Individuals with AI did about as well as teams without it.** Measured against individuals working alone without AI, solution quality rose by about a quarter of a standard deviation for teams without AI, and by more than a third for individuals with AI and for teams with AI. The authors describe individuals with AI as comparable to teams without it.

**AI narrowed the gap between kinds of expertise.** Without AI, commercial people proposed commercial ideas and technical people proposed technical ones. With AI, both produced more balanced proposals.

**The exceptional results came from teams with AI.** Among individuals working without AI, 5.8 percent of solutions ranked in the top tenth. Teams with AI were 9.2 percentage points more likely to produce one, a statistically significant difference. For individuals with AI the increase was 1.9 points, and for teams without AI 3.7 points, and neither was statistically significant.

The authors are careful about the limits, and so should a reader be. This was one day, one company, one industry and one AI model, with remote participants who mostly did not know each other and had little experience of prompting. The authors also report that AI-assisted solutions were more similar to one another, which raises a question about variety over time. A reader should also know the interests: Procter & Gamble supported the research institute involved, one author had consulted for the company and four of the authors were its employees.

What does this support? It supports testing different configurations of people and AI, because the old assumption that good work needs a team no longer holds automatically. It does not support removing half a department. A one-day exercise does not reproduce Client relationships, implementation, conflicting obligations, disruption or the slow accumulation of judgment.

And it carries a finding that cuts against the headcount reading. If the best outcomes came from teams with AI, then for work where exceptional results matter, the evidence favors keeping the team and equipping it. Some work may need fewer people. Some may gain most from giving the same people better tools. An honest comparison includes both.

## 6. Assured delegation

If execution is to be delegated, the delegation has to be something a responsible person can stand behind. I use a working term for that: assured delegation. It means assigning bounded work with evidence sufficient for the level of reliance proposed, controls in proportion to the consequences and a workable way to intervene.

Assured does not mean guaranteed. It means that reliance has a stated basis and stays open to challenge. The term adds no doctrine. It is **Informed Intent** applied to a redesign, and its record has the four elements that doctrine requires of any authorization: purpose, authority, boundaries, exit.

The risks are not hypothetical. The Generative AI Profile published by the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology lists twelve, among them confabulation, problems in the way people and AI systems are configured to work together, information security and the integration of components across a value chain. A successful demonstration is not grounds for unrestricted operating authority.

In practice a delegation should distinguish at least four permissions. Each is a verb, and the verb is what gets governed.

| Permission | What it allows | The boundary to specify |
|---|---|---|
| Prepare | Assemble evidence or draft an analysis | Sources, data access and what to do when information is missing |
| Recommend | Rank options or propose a course of action | Criteria, stated uncertainty and when to escalate |
| Execute | Make a permitted change or start a transaction | Limits, authorization and whether the action can be reversed |
| Commit | Bind the enterprise or state an authoritative position | Who may approve, on what evidence and within what mandate |

These are proposed design categories and not legal classifications. A workflow may combine them. Its authority should never expand merely because the system is able to perform the next step.

The companion map supplies a live example. The manufacturing plugin's connector to one enterprise system looks records up: that is Prepare. A separate plugin from the system's own vendor lets the AI release a purchase order and reschedule production once a buyer approves: that is Execute. The same morning's work, on the same screen, crosses a line that the organization ought to have drawn on purpose. Permission to prepare a Client report does not include permission to send it. Permission to find a billing discrepancy does not include permission to change where a payment goes.

A delegation record should make the purpose, the permitted and forbidden actions, the evidence required, the conditions for escalation and the stopping rule open to inspection. Where possible the permissions should be enforced by the surrounding system and not left to instructions in a prompt. The companion map explains why under the heading that a policy is not a control: an instruction can be edited, and a model can fail to follow it.

Review should follow consequence and evidence. Low-consequence, reversible actions may justify lighter review once tested. Consequential commitments may need direct approval. Some activities should stay human-executed because their quality cannot yet be assessed well enough to delegate them.

## 7. The economics follow the work

An AI workflow may remove drafting time while adding preparation, integration, review and exception handling. A credible business case follows the work wherever it moves.

For a proposed workflow, compare the full cost of delivery with today's baseline:

- Design, integration and maintenance
- Model, infrastructure and outside-service costs
- Human supervision and verification
- Rework, exceptions and putting things right for customers
- Continuity arrangements and the expertise that must be kept

Set those costs beside quality, turnaround time and value to the customer. Treat estimates of failure costs as uncertain assumptions; a number does not become a measurement by sitting in a spreadsheet.

Released capacity is also not the same as realized savings. An hour saved becomes valuable when it improves delivery, produces more useful output, removes an avoidable cost or serves another stated purpose. It does not turn into cash by itself.

The case should survive a less favorable scenario: lower volumes, heavier review, a change of provider, a higher exception rate. Severe harms and obligations that cannot be traded away need explicit limits. They must not vanish into an average.

One test is especially revealing. If a qualified reviewer has to reconstruct almost every output from the original evidence, how much work has been delegated? Sometimes real value remains, in better organization or a faster first draft. Sometimes the gain disappears. The answer has to come from the workflow's measured performance, which is the subject of [Lean AI](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/lean-ai.md): the unit that matters is the total cost of an outcome that was accepted, not of an output that was produced.

## 8. The competence that must survive

The most serious way for a smaller core to fail is quiet. The firm keeps nominal accountability and removes the knowledge, time and capacity needed to exercise it.

A named owner is necessary, and a name alone is not accountability. My plain-speech term for the test is AOTL, ass on the line: whose name is on the line before the run? In a redesign the question has a second half. Can the person whose name is on the line still carry it? A person who is named but holds no decision rights, no oversight and no power to intervene is a nominee, and nominating one is worse than naming nobody, because it manufactures the appearance of accountability.

The retained core must be able to recognize material errors, understand consequential exceptions, question the system's recommendations and decide when work must stop. It also needs the capacity to act when several exceptions arrive at once. A dashboard does not establish that competence. Neither does one knowledgeable person who is already fully occupied. There is a threshold here, and my book states it: past a certain ratio of agents to human oversight, the allocation of accountability is defective, because the named human can no longer exercise it.

For each critical capability, management should be able to answer five questions.

- Who can diagnose a plausible but materially wrong result?
- Who covers that responsibility during absence or overload?
- Which activities can continue during a provider outage?
- Which must pause because no safe alternative exists?
- How will the next generation of practitioners acquire the experience needed to supervise the work?

The last question decides whether the model lasts. Routine work is how professional judgment has always been formed. A firm that removes the routine work has removed its own apprenticeship, and it needs another learning design in its place: supervised practice, independent reconstruction of selected cases, structured investigation of failures. Whether those substitutes work should itself be tested. Quinn saw the same hazard in 2000 from the other side, warning that a company which stops taking part in the work can lose the knowledge it thought it was only buying in. Prahalad and Hamel's point returns here too. What was learned collectively is lost collectively, and usually without anyone deciding to lose it.

**The constraint is not how much work AI can perform. It is how much delegated work the organization can remain competent to direct.**

## 9. The people in the core, and the people outside it

A reader who comes to this piece from its companion will notice a tension. The companion puts people at the center of every plugin and makes three commitments to the person doing the work: time spent learning is credited as work, the first pass by hand is kept and nobody signs what they had no time to read. This piece asks how much of the organization is necessary. Do the two belong together?

They do, and the order of decisions is what joins them.

**The default test comes first.** Before any redesign, ask whether the same people, better equipped, would create more value. The evidence in Section 5 gives that option real weight: the best results came from teams with AI. Any exercise that cannot return the answer "keep the team" is not a test. It is a headcount target with a study attached.

**Staffing decisions come last.** They follow demonstrated capacity, service obligations and continuity. They do not precede them. A firm that announces the reduction and then goes looking for the workflow has reversed the order, and it will discover what it needed from the people it removed only after they are gone.

**The commitments hold in a small core, and they matter more there.** The fewer people who direct the work, the more each of them must understand it. Credited learning time, the first pass by hand and review time in the schedule are not kindnesses. In a smaller core they are the mechanism by which competence survives.

**Value moves unevenly, and that is a governance matter.** In May 2026 I wrote about a Korean manufacturer whose union ratified a settlement that paid one division from a pool tied to AI-driven demand while other divisions received the standard raise. I called it a governance story and not a compensation story. When AI concentrates value inside a firm, the bargain that held the firm together is renegotiated whether or not anyone admits it. A redesign that ignores who gains and who bears the cost has not avoided the negotiation. It has only chosen to have it later, on worse terms.

**The law has its own view, and it differs by country.** This piece is about organizational design. It is not advice on reducing a workforce, and the essay's tests do not discharge any legal duty. Three examples show the kind of obligation that attaches the moment a redesign becomes a staffing decision. Each is stated as an example of a kind, as read on 15 November 2026, and none is a statement of what applies to a particular employer.

- *United States.* The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act provides that an employer shall not order a plant closing or mass layoff until 60 days after written notice (29 U.S.C. § 2102(a), read; coverage and thresholds are defined in § 2101). State laws may add to it.
- *European Union.* Council Directive 98/59/EC requires an employer contemplating collective redundancies to begin consultations with workers' representatives in good time with a view to reaching an agreement, and to notify the competent public authority (Articles 2(1) and 3(1); both provisions read). The directive is implemented by each Member State's own law, which varies and may go further.
- *Republic of Korea.* Article 24 of the Labor Standards Act restricts dismissal for managerial reasons. As reported in practitioner sources, it requires an urgent managerial necessity, efforts to avoid dismissal, fair and reasonable selection criteria and notice to and good-faith consultation with the employees' representative at least 50 days beforehand (statute not read at source).

Collective agreements, works councils and individual contracts add further terms, and in some settings they decide whether the technology may be introduced at all. Whether the use of AI to select people for dismissal is itself regulated is a separate question that this piece does not address. Any organization that acts on this essay to change its headcount needs employment counsel in each country concerned, before the decision and not after it.

The point of setting this out is not to discourage redesign. It is to place it. The firm of the future may well be smaller. If it is, the people in it will carry more, the people who leave it are owed a lawful and decent process, and the decision that separates the two groups is one that a named human being answers for.

## 10. The doctrines, applied

Three doctrines govern this account, and they set the conditions for the model proposed here. They are given in their short forms, as stated in [DOCTRINE.md](https://github.com/rolldabones/rolldabones/blob/main/DOCTRINE.md).

> Slow AI: AI that cannot be governed, explained and audited is not deployed; the three tests are conjunctive, and the doctrine is a precondition, not a speed limit.

For a smaller core, this means that a staffing plan cannot count on benefits from a workflow that has not yet met those tests.

> Informed Intent: no AI system acts without prior authorization stating its purpose, authority, boundaries and exit, given by a human who meets the five authorizer conditions.

The five conditions are Knowledge, Evidence, Authority, Time and Permission. A core staffed too thinly for its people to have the knowledge and the time has undermined its own basis for delegating anything.

> Final Liability rests with the Human: every material outcome attaches to a named human owner with decision rights, oversight and the power to intervene.

These doctrines state organizational responsibilities. They are not a determination of legal liability in any matter.

The practical consequence is demanding. Delegation has to remain governable after the redesign, including during absence, overload and disruption. Controls that depend on people whose roles are being removed must be replaced and tested before management relies on the new arrangement.

## 11. A bounded example

Suppose an advisory firm wants to serve more Clients without growing its workforce in proportion. This is an illustration and not a reported case.

It begins with the outcome: a timely, evidence-backed assessment that helps the Client make a defined decision.

It then takes delivery apart. AI might organize approved source material, flag apparent inconsistencies, prepare a first draft and test the draft against a checklist. A qualified professional decides what the findings mean, resolves conflicting evidence and approves the advice. In the terms of Section 6, the AI prepares and recommends, and people execute and commit.

The firm starts in shadow operation. AI outputs are compared with the established process before they touch Client work. The tests include missing documents, conflicting instructions, misleading sources and plausible errors. Review time and correction effort are recorded beside output quality.

Only when the evidence supports a defined use does management authorize that use, and the authority stays specific: drafting permitted, external communication and final approval reserved.

Then the firm watches what happens. If exception handling absorbs the gains, it narrows or redesigns the workflow. If quality improves and staffing does not fall, the experiment may still be a commercial success, because the same people are now delivering better work.

Staffing decisions follow demonstrated capacity, service obligations and continuity. They come at the end.

## 12. An acceptance test for organizational redesign

A successful task demonstration is one input to a staffing decision. These tests ask whether the proposed organization can sustain the service that results.

| Test | Evidence to require before relying further |
|---|---|
| Customer value | Outcomes measured against the service promise, including quality and timeliness |
| Total economics | A baseline comparison that includes review, exceptions, maintenance and transition |
| Retained competence | A demonstrated ability to detect and resolve material errors |
| Authority | Defined permissions, approval rights and escalation paths |
| Capacity | Evidence that oversight and exception handling still work at peak demand |
| Resilience | A rehearsed response to the loss of a critical provider or a key person |
| Learning | A credible way to maintain and develop the expertise that supervision depends on |
| Lawful process | Advice taken, in each country concerned, on the duties owed before any role is changed or removed |

Set the thresholds before looking at the results. Otherwise an attractive demonstration quietly redefines success.

The central check on bias is the default test from Section 9: would management accept a result showing that keeping the same number of people and equipping them better creates more value? If not, the exercise is testing a target and not a hypothesis.

The hypothesis should be rejected or narrowed if verification stays prohibitively expensive, if expertise degrades, if service quality falls or if continuity rests on assumptions the firm cannot support.

## 13. Designing for value

Quinn explained why a firm need not perform every activity it depends on. Drucker kept attention on the customer and on the creation of value. AI makes it reasonable to ask whether strategically important work can be delivered with a different human structure.

The synthesis is a design proposition: an enterprise may keep command of a capability while delegating more of its execution.

In its strongest form that is a competent human core with a clear commercial purpose, access to great productive capability and the authority and evidence to govern its use. In its weakest form it is a shell with a name on it.

Smallness may follow. It should not lead.

The objective is the greatest sustainable value the enterprise can deliver to its customers, within obligations it can meet and risks it can govern. If the firm is becoming a protocol wrapped in liability, then the first question of design is the one I asked in May: who is allowed to change it?

## Questions for discussion

1. Take one revenue-producing capability in your organization. Separate the capability from its current staffing model. Which of the two have you actually been protecting?
2. For that capability, where does the inner line fall between directing the work and executing it? Who in the organization could draw it today?
3. Work out three alternatives against the tests in Section 12: equip the existing team, redesign selected workflows, redesign the retained core. What evidence would change which one you prefer?
4. Name the person whose name is on the line for the redesigned capability. During their absence, who is it?
5. How did your best people learn their judgment? Which of those experiences would the redesign remove, and what replaces them?
6. Who gains and who bears the cost if the redesign succeeds? Have they been told, and by whom?
7. Would your leadership accept the answer "keep everyone and equip them better"? How do you know?
