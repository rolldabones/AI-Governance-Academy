Artificial intelligence (AI) does not diminish the role of the Chief Legal Officer.

It makes the role more consequential.

An article on the CLO and AI, credited above, was the occasion for writing this down. It is a starting point and no more. This primer is how I would do the job, and it comes from having done the job.

My position is this.

The CLO's emerging role is not primarily to govern AI. It is to govern the human decisions, organizational systems and consequences surrounding AI.

That is what I call **Slow AI**.

Slow AI does not mean slow technology or slow adoption.

It means governed AI: visible, explainable and auditable by design, with human agency preserved where consequences matter.

The rule beneath it is blunt: AI that cannot be governed, explained and audited should not be deployed, however fast it would make the business.

The objective is not to stop AI.

It is to use AI deliberately enough that the organization can answer five questions:

1. What are we trying to accomplish?
2. What may the AI do?
3. Where are its boundaries?
4. How will we know whether it worked?
5. Which Human ultimately owns the outcome?

Everything else follows from those questions.

The framework beneath this primer is mine, and it is small. Three doctrines: Slow AI, **Informed Intent** and **Final Liability rests with the Human**. One method, the [Slow AI Kitchen](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen), with twelve steps, ten gates and one controlling rule: use AI at the speed of human verification, not at the speed of machine generation. And one question about the choices an organization keeps open, **GRCnext™**. It works with any model and under whatever standard the organization has adopted, whether ISO, COSO, NIST or another.

Sections 1 to 13 each end with one thing to ask to see. If the record exists, read it. If it does not, that is the finding.

Two people taught me how to do this job long before AI arrived. They come first, because they set the frame for everything after them.

## 1. Partner and guardian

My guide for the work of a General Counsel has always been Benjamin W. Heineman Jr., who led General Electric's legal function for nearly twenty years. His work generally, and two books especially, shaped my thinking and, more importantly, my practice as General Counsel: *High Performance with High Integrity* (Harvard Business Press, 2008) and *The Inside Counsel Revolution: Resolving the Partner-Guardian Tension* (Ankerwycke, 2016). His own [summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2016/03/29/the-inside-counsel-revolution) of the second is the place to start.

Heineman's central problem is the one AI now puts to every CLO.

The General Counsel is partner to the board, the chief executive and the business leaders. The General Counsel is also, and ultimately, guardian of the corporation. The two roles pull against each other. His answer is not to choose. It is to be both, because each is done badly without the other.

He also gave the profession its two questions. The first is whether it is legal. The ultimate one is: "Is it right?"

AI tests all of it.

A CLO who is only a partner waves the tools through and finds out later what they did. A CLO who is only a guardian becomes the approval desk, and the business routes around Legal. Shadow AI is what an organization gets when its guardian stops being a partner.

AI also adds a question that has to be answered before Heineman's two can be asked at all: do we know what it is doing? A use nobody can see, explain or reconstruct cannot be tested for legality, let alone for rightness. Answering that prior question is the work of Slow AI.

Heineman's goal for the corporation is the fusion of high performance with high integrity, and he is clear about where integrity comes from: leadership, incentives and processes built into operations, not rules and penalties alone. The same holds for AI. Governance that lives in a policy beside the work will lose to the work. Governance built into the work is how an organization gets AI that is safe enough, auditable enough and useful enough to put to work.

That is the partner-guardian resolution for AI, and it settles what the CLO should and should not do.

The CLO should not become the Chief AI Officer.

Nor should Legal become the enterprise AI approval desk.

Business functions should own business outcomes. Technology teams should own technical implementation. Security should own appropriate security controls. Privacy should govern personal information. Human Resources should remain responsible for employment processes. The board sets the risk appetite within which all of them operate.

The CLO has a different role.

Legal helps make the accountability architecture coherent.

Legal does not settle which risks are acceptable. The named owner of the use and the executive who sponsors the program do that, inside the appetite the board has set. Legal's job is to make sure the decision has an owner, a record and an exit.

For each material AI use, determine:

- Who owns the objective?
- Who owns the system?
- Who assesses the risk?
- Who authorizes deployment?
- Who monitors it?
- Who can stop it?
- Who keeps the evidence?
- Who answers for the outcome?

The Slow AI Kitchen already has [names](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen#roles) for the answers. Each function has an AI Owner accountable for outcomes in that function. AI Operators are trained and authorized to use specific tools at specific tiers. An Executive Sponsor sits at the top of the escalation chain.

If the answer is "the AI committee," keep asking.

Committees coordinate.

A named Human decides.

**Ask to see:** the role assignment matrix and the escalation chain to a named executive, which are the outputs of [Step 11](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen#step-11-staff-the-brigade-gate-9-competency-and-authorization-record).

## 2. Know what you know

The other influence is, perhaps ironically, Donald Rumsfeld.

The in-house lawyer has to understand the risk landscape before working on the risks in it. The most useful map I know was made famous at a United States Department of Defense news briefing on February 12, 2002. Asked about the lack of evidence linking the government of Iraq with the supply of weapons of mass destruction to terrorist groups, the Secretary of Defense sorted knowledge into three kinds. "There are known knowns," he began: the things we are sure of. Then the known unknowns, the gaps we can see. Then the unknown unknowns, the gaps we cannot. The last kind, he said, is the one that causes the difficulty.

That is three. The matrix has a fourth cell, the unknown knowns: what an organization knows somewhere and has not told itself. For the in-house lawyer it may be the most useful of the four.

Each cell calls for a different kind of work.

| | What it is | What it is for AI | The answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Known knowns** | Risks we know and understand | AI uses the organization has seen, with risks it understands | **Systems.** For the legal function, the systems for contracts, advisory work and projects, litigation, training and education. For AI, the authorization, the classification, the owner, the register and the record |
| **Unknown knowns** | What the organization knows somewhere and has not told itself | The tools people already use, the workarounds and the places where the real process has left the official one | **Communications**, especially across the internal silos |
| **Known unknowns** | What we know we do not know | How the next model will behave, how the law will land, what an agent does in a state nobody foresaw | **Training** for the lawyers and **education** for the internal Clients. For AI, add the test, run before reliance |
| **Unknown unknowns** | What we do not know we do not know | The failure nobody imagined | **No solution.** Only preparation: slack capacity kept ready, so that the resources are there when the situation arises |

For the unknown knowns and the known unknowns the work is the same. You are shifting them to known knowns.

For the unknown unknowns there is nothing to shift. You keep enough slack. Sometimes that means using the Pareto rule (80/20) on purpose, so that good enough is good enough and limited resources, which are time, money and patience, are not wasted attempting perfection at tasks that do not require it.

The rest of this primer follows the four cells.

**Ask to see:** the four cells, drawn on a whiteboard with the leadership team, with the organization's AI uses written into them. The cells that stay empty are the agenda.

*Known knowns. Where the organization knows what it is doing with AI, the work is to build the systems that hold it.*

## 3. Begin with Informed Intent

Most organizations begin their AI governance discussions too late.

They start with the model, vendor, policy or regulation.

Start instead with the decision to use AI.

I call this Informed Intent.

Before consequential AI is deployed, the authorization states four things. All four are required.

| Element | The question the authorization answers |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | What is the task, and why are we using AI for it? |
| **Authority** | What may the AI do, and who authorized it? |
| **Boundaries** | What may it not do? When must it stop, pause or return control to a human? |
| **Exit** | When does the authorization expire, what retracts it and what is the practical path to manual work or shutdown? |

An authorization missing its exit is not an authorization. It is a hope.

Two things travel with it. The Definition of Done says what an acceptable result is, written by a human before the work begins. The evidence says what actually happened.

The instrument is half of the doctrine. The signature is the other half.

An authorization counts only when the human giving it has the Knowledge, Evidence, Authority, Time and Permission to give it: an understanding of the specific action, the tests and residual risk in front of them, the institutional power to commit the organization, a realistic opportunity to consider it and the freedom to decline without retaliation.

A signature that fails any one of the five is paperwork.

This is deliberately simple.

Governance often fails because organizations cannot answer simple questions about what they are doing, who authorized it and what success or failure looks like.

**Ask to see:** the signed authorization for one AI system already in production. The form is [W10](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/consultant-workbook.md#w10-informed-intent-authorization) in the Consultant Workbook, and for an agent it is the [service ticket](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/take-out-delivery-and-vending-machines.md#write-the-ticket).

## 4. Govern the verb, not the noun

"Are we allowed to use this product?" is usually the wrong question.

Ask instead:

What are we allowing the system to do?

AI might:

summarize, draft, recommend, rank, predict, decide, communicate, execute, purchase, transfer or terminate.

Those verbs carry very different consequences.

Using a large language model to summarize a public document is fundamentally different from allowing an AI agent to negotiate a contract, reject an applicant, initiate a payment or alter a production system.

So classify AI by its verb.

Then examine two more things:

the boundary within which it operates and the consequence if it succeeds, fails or behaves unexpectedly.

My shorthand is:

**Govern the verb, the boundary and the consequence.**

The more consequential the verb, the stronger the governance should become.

Write the boundary as actions on particular resources. "Help with customer onboarding" is too broad to govern. "Read these case files, prepare sourced follow-ups and hold all sending for release" is something a human can authorize and a team can test.

The verb is also what survives a change of technology. Products are renamed and models are replaced. "May send" and "may not send" stay legible through all of it.

**Ask to see:** the permitted verbs for each material system, written as actions on named resources.

## 5. Name the Human principal

AI makes it remarkably easy for responsibility to become diffuse.

The model recommended it.

The system generated it.

The algorithm ranked it.

The agent did it.

Those statements may describe what happened.

They do not answer who was responsible for allowing it to happen.

Somewhere in every consequential chain of action there should be an identifiable Human principal with decision rights, oversight and the power to intervene, named before the work begins.

In the Slow AI Kitchen this is Gate 5. If no one owns the outcome, the process stops. And the Owner cannot be the AI.

This is Final Liability.

Final Liability rests with the Human.

This is a governance principle, not a statement about the legal allocation of liability in every jurisdiction or factual circumstance. Legal liability will depend on applicable law, contracts, corporate structures and the facts.

The governance principle is simpler:

Responsibility must not disappear into the machinery.

For every material AI use, someone should have the authority and practical ability to say:

Yes.

No.

Stop.

Escalate.

If nobody can, the system is not adequately governed.

A name alone is not enough. A person who is named but holds no decision rights, no oversight and no power to intervene is a nominee, and a nominee is worse than a blank: it manufactures the appearance of accountability.

Accountability also has three moments. The owner is named at specification. Accountability attaches when that person confirms the output against criteria set in advance. It holds for as long as anyone relies on the result.

And owners leave. Every owner needs a deputy who has done the job at least once, and a vacancy that runs past a set limit suspends the system instead of leaving it unowned.

One part of naming the owner is the CLO's own craft: the contract.

The vendor is not the owner. A vendor can assume contractual risk between the parties. It cannot assume a duty the organization owes to a regulator.

So reserve liability language for where legal liability genuinely lands. Do not write that responsibility is "shared" without saying how it is divided and what happens when the division is contested. And do not let an indemnity stand in for an owner.

Divide ownership by layer instead. The vendor owns the model. The organization owns the version it runs, the instructions it gives, the tools and permissions it grants, the knowledge it retrieves from and the behavior of all of it in context. The Architecture Primer sets out the [layers](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/primer/05-part-4-governance-by-design.md#ownership-when-the-vendor-owns-the-model).

**Ask to see:** the named Owner of each Tier 3 use, the deputy and the last time either of them stopped something. The record is [W04](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/consultant-workbook.md#w04-accountability-and-oversight).

## 6. Make the invisible visible

You cannot govern what you cannot see.

Maintain an AI register. In the Slow AI Kitchen this is the tool registry of [Step 10](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen#step-10-plan-the-menu-gate-8-authorized-tool-registry): authorized systems, permitted uses, tier assignments and prohibited uses for each function.

For each material use, a CLO should be able to read off:

**System → Use Case → Verb → Owner → Data → Permissions → Consequence → Controls → Human Decision Point → Evidence → Exit → Status**

Add one column that is easy to leave out: the model and version the system depends on, with the date that dependency ends.

Do not inventory only products labeled "AI."

AI increasingly arrives embedded inside productivity software, recruitment systems, customer platforms, cybersecurity tools, analytics, coding environments and professional applications.

Nor should the register become an administrative monument.

Its purpose is operational.

At any material point in the system, you should be able to answer:

What is this AI doing, under whose authority and subject to what controls?

**Ask to see:** the register, sorted by tier, with the date each entry was last reviewed. The record for each tool is [W06](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/consultant-workbook.md#w06-authorized-tool-and-data-record).

## 7. Build evidence into the system

A policy saying that humans remain accountable is not evidence that a human actually exercised accountability.

For consequential AI, preserve enough evidence to reconstruct the decision:

**Intent → Authority → Input → AI Action → Control → Human Decision → Outcome**

The organization should be able to answer later:

- What happened?
- Why did it happen?
- Who authorized it?
- What did the system do, and on which model and version?
- What did the Human decide?
- What evidence was available?
- What controls operated?
- What changed afterward?
- Can we explain the outcome to the person it affected, in terms that person can use?

A governance system is proven by what it can reconstruct after a failure, not by the principles it has published.

Three points are the CLO's to press.

An audit log is written for a reader who does not trust you: an auditor, a regulator, opposing counsel, the person affected. Engineering traces are sampled, editable and short-lived. A team with tracing does not thereby have an audit log.

The evidence has to outlive the system. Normal operation destroys three things before anyone thinks of them: the knowledge base as it stood on the day, short-retention logs and the model version, which the provider may retire. A [legal hold](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/primer/05-part-4-governance-by-design.md#legal-hold-applied-to-ai-artifacts) has to be able to suspend the deletion of each, and that is decided when the system is built.

And the evidence should sit where the organization controls it, not only inside the vendor's product.

This is why governance is not principally a documentation exercise.

It is a decision system that produces proof as it runs.

Good governance leaves evidence behind, and the evidence exists before it is asked for, not after.

**Ask to see:** one consequential decision from last quarter, reconstructed end to end from the record alone. The register is [W14](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/consultant-workbook.md#w14-evidence-register-and-decision-pack).

## 8. Ask, Look & Listen

Do not begin with a 70-page AI policy.

Begin with reality.

**Ask.**

What AI are people actually using? For what? With what information? Where are they already finding value? What worries them?

**Look.**

Examine systems, permissions, contracts, workflows, integrations, logs, outputs, incidents and actual behavior.

**Listen.**

Pay attention to the people closest to the work. They usually know where the official process and the real process diverge.

This is the unknown known. The organization already knows what AI it is using. The knowledge sits in the silos: in the business teams, in Technology, Security, Privacy, Procurement and Human Resources, each holding a piece and none holding the whole. Nobody has asked, so nobody has said. Communication is the instrument, and it runs in both directions. People tell Legal what they are doing when Legal has shown that telling leads to help, not to a ban.

AI adoption frequently precedes AI governance.

That means the first AI inventory is partly an exercise in organizational anthropology.

Find out what is actually happening before writing rules about what you imagine is happening.

**Ask to see:** nothing yet. Go and look. Sit with three teams for an hour each before reading a single policy.

## 9. Agents change the governance equation

There is an important difference between AI that produces something and AI that does something.

An assistant drafts.

An agent acts.

As AI systems acquire tools, memory, permissions and access to other systems, the governance question changes.

It is no longer merely:

Is the output accurate?

It becomes:

What can this thing actually do, and what may happen between human checkpoints?

For every agent, ask:

- What can it see?
- What can it change?
- Who or what can it contact?
- What can it spend or commit?
- Which decisions can it make?
- Which decisions require human approval?
- What stops it?
- What gets logged?
- Who reviews those logs?

Agent designs will keep changing. One agent becomes several. A run becomes a standing service. A tool connection appears that did not exist last quarter. Four tests hold through all of it, and each has evidence a CLO can ask for before the system goes live.

| The agent must be | The evidence |
|---|---|
| **Bounded** | An out-of-scope action is refused by access controls, not merely discouraged by instructions. Limits on time, cost and actions cover the whole run |
| **Observable** | One run identifier joins the authorization, the sources, the human decisions and every tool action, including the blocked ones |
| **Interruptible** | A trial has shown the stop working, including work already in flight, and measured how long it took |
| **Attributable** | A named Owner has the decision rights, access to the record and practical power to intervene |

Five rules follow, and none depends on the technology of the day.

1. A tool's presence does not authorize every use of it.
2. Instructions tell the agent what to do. Access controls decide what it can do. Put the restriction where the action occurs.
3. Content the agent encounters cannot grant it authority. A document, an email or another agent's message that says "send this" is information, not permission.
4. Delegation stays inside the authorization. Where one agent hands work to others, the limits, the expiry and the stop reach every one of them.
5. Memory carried from an earlier session is context to check. It is not continuing authority.

Count consequences across related agents and runs. Splitting work into smaller pieces does not lower its aggregate impact.

This is where "govern the verb" becomes particularly important.

An agent's risk is not defined merely by its intelligence.

It is defined by its capabilities, permissions and consequences.

**Ask to see:** the service ticket for one agent and the receipt for one of its runs. Both are in [Take-Out, Delivery & Vending Machines](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/take-out-delivery-and-vending-machines.md), with the [tests](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/take-out-delivery-and-vending-machines.md#test-what-travels).

## 10. Govern change as well as state

AI does not stand still.

Models change.

Vendors change.

Data changes.

Prompts change.

Integrations change.

Permissions change.

Employees discover new uses.

Agents acquire new tools.

A system approved six months ago may no longer be the system operating today.

Governance therefore needs to address both state and change.

**Hold constant what the technology cannot change.** The verb, the boundary, the consequence, the evidence and the named Human. A new model does not alter what the organization permitted. A new agent design does not alter who answers for the result.

**Treat an approval as attaching to a configuration, not a product.** What was authorized is a particular combination: the instructions, the model and its version, the tools and their permissions, the knowledge it retrieves from, the routing, the guardrails and the tests it passed. The Architecture Primer calls this the [release tuple](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/primer/03-2-9-the-ai-architecture-decision-record-to-solution.md#rollback-when-the-artifact-is-a-prompt-and-a-model-version). Change one field and the thing that was approved no longer exists in the form in which it was approved.

Three kinds of change deserve a CLO's attention, because each arrives looking like a technical upgrade.

| What changes | What it does to the authorization | What to require |
|---|---|---|
| **The model.** A new version, a provider-side update or a retirement notice | Behavior changes, and a newer model can be worse at your task. A pinned version only postpones the change to a date the vendor sets | Tests on your own work, with the pass mark set before the run. A dependency register with a named migration owner. Notice of change and retirement in the contract |
| **The agent.** A new tool or connection, memory, more agents, a schedule or trigger, a wider destination | The verb has changed. Drafting became sending. One run became a standing service | A new or amended service ticket. A tier check, because automation tied to action is Tier 3. Limits that reach every delegated agent |
| **The architecture.** A new knowledge source, a routing change, a new region or point of entry, a second provider | Who can reach what has changed, and with it confidentiality, privilege, residency and concentration | A release record covering every field. A way back that needs no rebuild. The privilege and residency questions asked before the change, not after |

Nine events reopen the question. Each should be a monitored event, not a policy sentence:

1. a new tool added to an agent's allowlist
2. a change in the class of data the system can reach
3. extension to a new user population or jurisdiction
4. a change in the model or its version by the provider
5. an incident of any severity
6. a material change in the regulatory character of the use
7. a change in the recorded purpose of the system or its data
8. the departure of the named Owner of a high-stakes use
9. the lapse of the review period

The list is the Architecture Primer's set of [re-tiering triggers](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/primer/05-part-4-governance-by-design.md#tier-drift-self-tiering-and-re-tiering-triggers).

Four rules keep this honest.

**No silent change.** No increase in tier or scope without a decision on the record. No prompt, model, tool, knowledge or guardrail change ships without a test run.

**Set the pass mark before the test.** A threshold chosen after the results arrive is not a standard. It is acceptance criteria written to fit the result. The gate for a [model swap](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/primer/02-part-2-solution-architecture-to-2-8-entry-points-and.md#the-eval-gate-on-a-model-swap) is a test set drawn from real work, a way of grading it and a threshold fixed in advance.

**A setting that removes a human checkpoint is a control change.** It needs the approval that removing the control would need, however small the technical change looks.

**An exit nobody has rehearsed is a document.** The model provider is a critical third party. Decide by tier how quickly the organization must be able to substitute it, and note that the test set is what makes substitution possible: without one, nobody can say whether the substitute is acceptable. A pinned version without an expiry date and a named owner is a deferral, not a control. The Architecture Primer covers [retirement](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/primer/03-2-9-the-ai-architecture-decision-record-to-solution.md#model-deprecation-as-a-standing-architectural-risk) and [substitution](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/primer/05-part-4-governance-by-design.md#concentration-and-substitutability).

Two matters here belong on the CLO's own desk.

**The contract and the configuration.** The contract should say what the vendor will tell you and when: a change of model, a retirement, a new subprocessor, an incident. It should say what happens to your data: retention, training use, residency. It should say what you can take with you: logs, records and help on the way out. Then ask the question almost nobody asks. The contract says zero retention. The production setting was chosen by an engineer eighteen months ago. Who owns the assertion that the two match, and when was it last tested?

**The regulatory role.** Most AI regulation assigns duties by role, and the role follows what the organization does, not what it calls itself. Four ordinary business decisions can move an organization into a heavier role without anyone deciding that it should: putting its own name on the system, using it for a purpose the vendor did not intend, modifying the model and supplying the system to another party. None of them looks like a legal act. Ask to be told when any of them is proposed. Whether the role has changed is a legal determination, jurisdiction by jurisdiction. It is the CLO's question, and the facts that answer it are known only to the people building the system.

This is why annual AI policies alone are insufficient.

AI governance must become an operating process.

**Ask to see:** the last three changes to a Tier 3 system and the reauthorization record for each, which is [W20](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/consultant-workbook.md#w20-change-and-reauthorization-record). If changes shipped without one, the approvals on file describe systems that no longer exist.

## 11. Start inside Legal

The legal department should be an early proving ground.

Map where lawyers are already using AI:

research, drafting, discovery, contract analysis, due diligence, investigations, compliance, knowledge management and outside-counsel management.

Then apply exactly the same questions.

- What is the task?
- What is the Definition of Done?
- What information may enter the system?
- What may the AI do?
- What requires lawyer review?
- What evidence should be retained?
- When must the lawyer stop or escalate?
- Who owns the final work product?

For lawyers the verb also carries the duty. The rules of professional conduct already classify work by consequence, and the AI classification should land in the same places.

Privilege needs its own attention. Confidentiality and privilege are different obligations, and a design that protects the first does not automatically protect the second. Whether routing content through a vendor bears on privilege is a legal question in each jurisdiction where the organization may litigate. The facts that shape the answer are configuration facts: retention, training use, the subprocessor chain, where processing happens and who at the vendor can read the content. The Architecture Primer sets out those [design choices](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/primer/05-part-4-governance-by-design.md#413-privilege-and-confidentiality) and the questions to settle.

Record the purpose and the direction of AI-assisted legal work at the time, not afterward. And treat the instructions lawyers write for these systems as sensitive: they can reflect strategy.

Then train and educate, which are different jobs. The lawyers need training: enough understanding of how these systems work and fail to supervise them competently, not nominally. The internal Clients need education: what they may do, what they may not and when to call. Treat both as a long-term investment in capacity and capability. Give people the time and give them credit for the time. The [workshop](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen#the-workshop) teaches the method on real tasks.

Legal should demonstrate governed adoption, not merely prescribe it to everyone else.

**Ask to see:** the legal department's own entries in the register and one piece of AI-assisted legal work with its review record. The build detail for a legal organization is in the [Law Firm AI Deployment Spec v2](law-firm-ai-deployment-spec.md), and the verification discipline is in [*Sounds Good*](sounds-good.md).

*Unknown unknowns. There is no solution. There is slack.*

## 12. Put friction where the consequences are

Slow AI is not an argument for making everything slow.

Slow does not mean uniform friction. It means care at the joints, where judgment and authorization sit, and free movement everywhere else.

Low-consequence activity should be easy.

Consequential activity should be deliberate.

The Slow AI Kitchen's three [risk tiers](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen#risk-tiers) do this work, with the registry's prohibited list beside them.

| Tier | What it covers | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1: Low-Stakes Work** | Rough brainstorming, non-public internal drafts, formatting and idea generation | Abbreviated Definition of Done and basic human review. Nothing leaves the organization unverified |
| **Tier 2: Moderate-Stakes Work** | Internal analyses, training materials, Client-facing drafts under review and process documentation | Full Definition of Done, a context check, a named reviewer and an evidence log |
| **Tier 3: High-Stakes Work** | Decisions affecting customers, employees or regulated operations, legal, compliance or risk determinations, external representations and automation tied to action | The full method, an explicit escalation path, second review, a sign-off record and a remediation or rollback plan |
| **Prohibited** | Activity outside legal authority or organizational risk appetite | Not run. Escalate or redesign |

Two cautions belong to the CLO.

The team that scores a system is usually the team whose delivery date depends on a low score. Sample the scores independently, and let certain facts set the tier without any scoring at all: automation tied to action is Tier 3, whatever the score.

And an internal tier is not a legal classification. Keep the two in separate fields. A regulator classifies by what a system is for. The tier classifies by what it can do. Both are correct for their purpose, and neither answers the other's question.

Governance should impose friction in proportion to consequence.

Everything else is bureaucracy.

This is the Pareto rule put to work. Good enough is good enough, and where the consequences are small it is the standard to hold. Every hour of review spent perfecting a low-stakes task is an hour of slack the organization will not have when something it never imagined arrives.

**Ask to see:** the Tier 3 list, who assigned each tier and how many of those assignments were checked by someone outside the requesting team.

## 13. Design for failure

AI will fail.

So will humans.

Some of those failures will be ones nobody imagined. For the unknown unknown there is no solution. There is only preparation: enough slack held ready that the resources exist when the situation arrives. In AI, slack has specific forms. Reviewers with time to review. A manual path that still exists, with people still trained on it. An owner who is not too busy to intervene. A stop that has been rehearsed. A way out that has been rehearsed.

Governance is partly the discipline of preventing ordinary failures from becoming extraordinary ones.

For material systems, establish the ability to:

**detect → pause → contain → investigate → decide → remediate → document → improve**

Do not merely ask whether someone theoretically has authority to stop the system.

Ask whether that person knows they have the authority, can recognize when intervention is required and can actually exercise it in time.

A stop button nobody can find is decoration.

A decision right nobody will exercise is not a control.

Stopping an agent is also not the same as undoing what it did. A request already accepted by another system may still complete. A message already sent cannot be recalled. The [procedure](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/take-out-delivery-and-vending-machines.md#stop-reconcile-and-hand-back) is contain, preserve, reconcile, recover and hand back, and a timeout does not prove failure.

Every AI-dependent process also needs a defined answer to what happens when the AI is unavailable, wrong or too slow: reduced capability, human-only or suspended.

Design the exit as well as the stop. In GRCnext™ terms the stop is a Switch and the way out is an Exit, and both are built and tested before they are needed. GRCnext™ asks the question traditional governance, risk management and compliance tends to skip: what choices will we still have when the risk happens?

**Ask to see:** the record of the last stop drill, with the time it took. The exercise is [W21](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/consultant-workbook.md#w21-stop-and-fallback-exercise). Run it on a model change and an absent owner, not only on an outage.

*Putting it to work.*

## 14. The CLO's first 90 days

Do not begin by trying to build the perfect AI governance framework.

Build a functioning one.

**Days 1–30: Ask, Look & Listen** (unknown knowns into known knowns)

- Identify actual AI use.
- Find the important systems, shadow uses and existing controls.
- Determine where consequential decisions are already being augmented or automated.
- Start the AI register as a working inventory.
- Identify the obvious gaps.

*By day 30:* a working inventory, a list of the consequential decisions AI already touches and a short list of gaps the sponsor must decide.

**Days 31–60: Establish Informed Intent** (systems for the known knowns)

- Classify material use cases by tier.
- Name owners and deputies.
- Define permitted uses and boundaries.
- Establish approval and escalation thresholds.
- Identify prohibited uses.
- Define human decision points, intervention rights and exits.
- Choose one valuable workflow and take it through the method properly.

*By day 60:* a tiered register with its prohibited list, a role assignment matrix, an escalation chain to a named executive and signed authorizations for the Tier 3 uses.

**Days 61–90: Build the operating system** (tests for the known unknowns, slack for the rest)

- Bring the AI register into operation, with owners, evidence and status kept current.
- Implement proportionate approval and evidence requirements.
- Establish monitoring and incident procedures.
- Start the model dependency register and the change record.
- Train people on the rules.
- Test escalation.
- Test the evidence trail.
- And, critically, test whether the organization can actually stop a consequential AI process.

*By day 90:* an evidence register, an incident procedure, a change record in use, one timed stop drill, one decision reconstructed from the record and a first report to the board.

The one workflow matters more than the framework. The [Consultant Workbook](https://github.com/rolldabones/slow-ai-kitchen/blob/main/consultant-workbook.md) carries a single priority workflow from baseline to a decision on evidence in 30 days, and it fits inside this plan. It gives the organization something the framework cannot: proof on its own work.

Then improve it.

The objective is not perfection.

It is a governance system that works.

## 15. The Slow AI test

Before consequential AI is deployed, the CLO should be able to ask the five opening questions at full strength and receive five clear answers:

1. What are we trying to accomplish?
2. What exactly may the AI do, and on whose authority?
3. Where are its boundaries, and what is the exit?
4. What evidence will tell us whether it is working as intended?
5. Which named Human owns the outcome, with the decision rights, oversight and power to intervene?

And one more, asked again every time the system changes:

6. Is the thing running today the thing that was approved?

If those questions cannot be answered, the organization may not yet have an AI problem.

It has a governance problem.

## The architecture

At its simplest, Slow AI creates a chain:

**Informed Intent → Governed Action → Evidence → Human Judgment → Final Liability**

Informed Intent establishes what we are trying to do.

Governed Action defines what the AI may actually do.

Evidence makes the system observable and auditable.

Human Judgment preserves the capacity to choose and intervene.

Final Liability prevents accountability from disappearing into the machine.

The CLO does not need to become a machine-learning engineer to lead this work.

But the CLO does need to understand AI well enough to govern the consequential human choices made around it.

That is the opportunity.

Not slower innovation.

Not compliance theater.

Not another committee.

AI that is safe enough, auditable enough and useful enough to put to work.

And an organization that remains capable of understanding what it is doing, changing course when necessary and answering for the consequences.

**Govern the verb. Govern the boundary. Govern the consequence. Name the Human.**
