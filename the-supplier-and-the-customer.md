# The Supplier and the Customer

**Choosing whether and how to build open or closed AI models into a product or service: a unit on strategy**

Son-U Michael Paik · CEO, GRC Solutions Korea

22 November 2026

**Starting point.** If Anthropic were in Shenzhen, what would we call Claude Design? Writing from Korea, I suspect many readers would reach for *reverse engineering*, and would mean it as an accusation. The public record contains no account of anything taken apart. It shows a supplier whose model powered a customer's product, a customer success story the supplier published and then a supplier product that offers part of the same job directly. Everything in that sequence can be lawful. This unit asks what the sequence means for anyone deciding whether to build a business on someone else's artificial intelligence (AI), and whether we would describe the same facts in the same words whatever the supplier's address.

**For:** product owners, founders, finance leads, general counsel and the person accountable for a customer outcome, before they commit a product or service to an AI model. Some familiarity with how AI services are bought and run is assumed; [*Systems Architecture for AI*](systems-architecture-for-ai.md) is the introduction if it is not.

**What this unit is and is not.** It is a method for one strategic decision: which model route, if any, a product or service should depend on, and what the business still sells if the supplier competes with it. It is not a model comparison, a procurement checklist or a statement of law. Where it mentions law it names the jurisdiction and the date, and it decides no reader's case. It adds no doctrine, step, gate, tier or form to this account; the exercise uses the five GRCnext™ questions as they already stand.

**How to use it.** Read Sections 1 to 8 once (about 55 minutes). Then run the exercise in Section 10 with three people: the product owner, the finance lead and the person accountable for the customer outcome (60 minutes, debrief included). Section 11 holds discussion questions for a longer session.

## In brief

1. Start with the customer, not the model. Finish this sentence in words a customer would recognize: *Even if our supplier offers this feature tomorrow, you will still hire us to ____.* If you cannot, no model route will save the business.
2. "Open or closed?" is one question inside three: **rights** (what you may run, change, ship and keep), **operations** (who serves the model, sees the data, fixes it and chooses the next version) and **distribution** (where the customer starts work and who answers when the result is wrong).
3. A model supplier can see which jobs its customers' customers value. A connector or marketplace listing can be a new channel and a substitute in the same quarter. Measure which. Turning a model on someone else's product is governed by older rules: lawful reverse engineering, yes; misrepresented access, breached terms or copied expression, no.
4. Access can end for reasons no commercial contract covers. In June 2026 one supplier withdrew two models from every customer under a government directive; access returned nineteen days later, unevenly across channels. Hold an exit you have rehearsed, not one you have described.
5. Compare routes on the cost of an accepted result and on the contribution each customer pays at renewal. A timed review of real work can overturn an infrastructure business case before anyone buys a server.

## 1. The question before the model

A firm can rent extraordinary capability and build a durable business around it. A firm can also run its own model and discover that it owns the machines on which an undistinguished product runs. Neither outcome is decided by the model route. Both are decided by what the customer is buying.

Consider two buyers of a design tool. A founder needs a prototype for Friday's pitch. A design director needs six teams to maintain one design system through a year of releases. A direct route from prompt to prototype may satisfy the founder entirely. The director may care more about what happens after the prototype: who can change a component, how the team reviews competing versions and whether engineering receives an approved design. Losing the first buyer and keeping the second are both plausible. Neither is established by a supplier's launch announcement.

The strategic unit of analysis is therefore the customer's job, stated as the result the customer accepts. Write it down before reading any model card. Then write the strongest direct substitute for it, the supplier's own product included. If the substitute does the whole job at a lower price, the model route is a cost question for a business in decline.

## 2. What the supplier can see

**The design case.** Anthropic's [customer story on Figma](https://claude.com/customers/figma) says Claude powers Figma Make, which builds interactive prototypes from descriptions or existing Figma designs, and cites Figma's chief executive, Dylan Field, on the round trip between Figma Design and Make. On 13 January 2026 Anthropic [announced](https://www.anthropic.com/news/introducing-anthropic-labs) that Mike Krieger, its Chief Product Officer for the previous two years, was moving to Labs, the team that incubates experimental products. On 14 April 2026 Figma reported in a [Form 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1579878/000162828026025127/fig-20260414.htm) that Mr. Krieger had resigned from its board, effective immediately, and that his decision was not due to any disagreement with the company on any matter related to its operations, policies or practices. On 17 April 2026 Anthropic Labs [introduced Claude Design](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-design-anthropic-labs) as a research preview for paid Claude plans, quoting Canva's co-founder and chief executive and offering export to Canva. The announcement does not mention Figma. None of these documents says how Claude Design was developed, and this unit draws no inference about it.

**The legal case.** On 12 May 2026 Anthropic [announced](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-for-the-legal-industry) 12 practice-area plugins and more than 20 new connectors for legal work, describing legal professionals as the most engaged Claude Cowork users of any knowledge-work function. A model supplier can see that demand in its own product. In the same announcement Legora's chief technology officer, Jake Lauritzen, put the division of labor plainly: Anthropic builds the intelligence and Legora turns it into production systems. Harvey appears as a connector that brings its legal tools into Claude.

**What the two cases teach.** For a specialist, that connector can be an excellent distribution agreement: a lawyer who would never open the specialist's application can now reach its service. It can also let an existing customer move routine work into the supplier's product, call the specialist occasionally and ask for fewer seats at renewal. Both effects can occur in the same quarter. Connector calls alone cannot tell management which business it is building. Count, for each route, new paying customers, revenue retained at renewal and contribution earned.

## 3. What the Korean analogy explains

GoldStar's A-501 gives the comparison a physical object. Korea's [heritage register](https://digital.khs.go.kr/heri/heriDetail.do?ctptNo=4413105590100&ctptUid=13983497625068030519) records it as the country's first vacuum-tube radio, made in 1959. Wonhyuk Lim's [history of the industry's formative years](https://audiopub.co.kr/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/The-Development-of-Koreas-Electronics-Industry-During-Its-Formative-Years-1966-1979-English.pdf) says GoldStar developed it on the basis of a Japanese Sanyo model. Its designer, Kim Hae-soo, had learned radios by assembling and repairing them while running a radio shop. GoldStar released it on 15 November 1959, with about a third of its components imported. Here we can name the reference product, the engineer, the result and the share that was not yet made at home. The Figma record supplies no comparable account of a teardown. Under United States federal trade-secret law, reverse engineering and independent derivation are expressly excluded from "improper means" ([18 U.S.C. § 1839(6)(B)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-142/t18/s1839/)). A claim of wrongdoing needs its own facts and its own applicable law.

Michael Hobday [traced](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/video/sussexlectures/pdf/michaelhobday.pdf) how electronics firms in Korea and Taiwan learned through subcontracting for foreign buyers, who advised on equipment, engineering, training and management. Over time some built their own product designs, brands and distribution, while remaining weak, he noted, in basic research. He put the effort at 20 to 30 years and cautioned that not all firms followed the same stages. The customer was the supplier's school.

A frontier model supplier approaches the customer from the other direction. It begins at the technological frontier, and what it can learn downstream is which jobs people value enough to do repeatedly. Korea explains how the bargaining positions of a buyer and a supplier can change while both do exactly what they agreed to do. Shenzhen tests our vocabulary.

**Apply one evidentiary standard to provenance.** The same discipline applies when a model is chosen or rejected because of where it came from. On 23 February 2026 Anthropic [described distillation](https://www.anthropic.com/news/detecting-and-preventing-distillation-attacks), training one model on another's outputs, as a widely used and legitimate training method, and alleged that DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax had generated more than 16 million exchanges through about 24,000 fraudulent accounts in violation of its terms of service and regional access restrictions. Those are one party's allegations. In *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, the United States District Court for the Northern District of California [held on 23 June 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-cand-3_24-cv-05417/pdf/USCOURTS-cand-3_24-cv-05417-0.pdf) that the training use before it was fair use and that converting purchased print books to digital form was fair use for a different reason, but denied Anthropic summary judgment that the pirated copies in its central library must be treated as training copies, and set those copies for trial. On 20 July 2026 the court [granted final approval](https://www.anthropiccopyrightsettlement.com/documents) of a US$1.5 billion class settlement. Training, acquiring copies and obtaining access under false pretenses are distinct acts. Judge each on its evidence, whichever company, author or country is on the other side.

For procurement, three questions follow, and neither downloadable weights nor a paid subscription answers them: how were the inputs acquired, what use is permitted and who bears the cost if those rights are challenged?

## 4. What open and closed actually decide

Take one workflow and draw three boundaries. **Rights** determine what you may run, modify, distribute or retain. **Operations** determine who serves the model, sees the inputs, fixes failures and chooses the next version. **Distribution** determines where the customer starts work, who invoices the customer and who gets called when the result is wrong. "Open or closed?" answers only part of the first.

**Terms.** An *open-weight* release makes the model's learned parameters downloadable under stated license terms. That is narrower than *open source* as the Open Source Initiative [defines it for AI](https://opensource.org/ai/open-source-ai-definition) (version 1.0, 28 October 2024): the freedoms to use, study, modify and share the system, with the parameters, the complete code and enough information about the training data for a skilled person to build a substantially equivalent system. OpenAI, for example, [describes](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-oss/) its gpt-oss models (5 August 2025) as open-weight releases under the Apache 2.0 license. A *closed* model is reached only as a service, usually through an application programming interface (API), on the supplier's terms. A company can serve open weights itself or pay a host to serve them; the host is then a closed supplier of operations, even though the weights are open. A company can also put a closed model behind its own product and keep the direct customer relationship. Buying a model service and distributing through the supplier's own application are separate decisions.

**The question bank.** Ask each question of every route under consideration. A blank answer is a finding.

| Boundary | Ask of an open-weight route | Ask of a closed service |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rights: use | Does the license for this exact release permit our use, our customers' use and any user or field restrictions? Does it pass to our customers if we ship the model? | Does the governing agreement permit our use case and our customers' use? Which acceptable-use terms can change, and on what notice? |
| Rights: data | Where do prompts, outputs and logs go once we or our host run it? | May the supplier retain or use our inputs and outputs, for how long and for what? Where is data processed? |
| Rights: risk transfer | Who stands behind the weights? Permissive licenses commonly disclaim warranties and limit liability; who bears an infringement claim? | Does the supplier offer an indemnity, for which claims, with which exclusions and caps? |
| Operations: change | Can we fix the version indefinitely? Who patches a discovered weakness, and how do we learn of it? | What notice do we get before a model is changed or retired? Can we pin a version, and for how long? What does revalidation cost us each time? |
| Operations: capacity | Can we meet peak demand, recover from failure and staff the service? | What rate limits, regional availability and service levels apply, and what remedy follows a breach? |
| Operations: portability | How much of our prompts, tools and evaluation set is tuned to this model? | The same question, and whether the supplier's own features (tools, memory, file handling) have an equivalent elsewhere. |
| Distribution | Does running it ourselves win customers who require local processing? | Does the supplier sell the same job to our customers, inside a subscription they already hold? |
| Regulatory role | If we fine-tune, modify or redistribute, do we become the provider of a model or system under the law that applies to us? What documentation is the upstream provider obliged to give us? | Which obligations stay with the supplier, which pass to us as deployer or downstream provider and what documentation does the supplier give us to meet them? |

On the regulatory row, one example of why the label matters to the law and does not settle it. Under the European Union's AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, [Article 53(2)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:02024R1689-20260727) disapplies two duties of a general-purpose AI model provider, the technical documentation of Article 53(1)(a) and the information for downstream providers of Article 53(1)(b), where the model is released under a free and open-source license allowing access, use, modification and distribution and its parameters, architecture and usage information are public. The relief does not reach models with systemic risk, and the duties on copyright policy and the training-content summary still apply. The consolidated text of 27 July 2026, which incorporates the amendments of Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, carries that paragraph in its original wording. Two consequences follow for a buyer. A firm that modifies such a model may itself become a provider; the Commission's guidelines of 18 July 2025 [address](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/learn-more-about-guidelines-providers-general-purpose-ai-models) when that happens. And an upstream provider that qualifies for the relief owes its downstream integrators less information by statute than one that does not, so the documentation a closed supplier gives by obligation may have to be obtained some other way on an open route. Confirm both points with counsel for the facts of your case.

## 5. When access ends

On 12 June 2026 Anthropic [announced](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access) that a United States export control directive, received at 5:21 pm Eastern Time that day, suspended all access to Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 by foreign nationals, inside or outside the United States, and that to comply it must disable both models for all its customers. Its [later notice](https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5) explains why a restriction on one class of user became a withdrawal for everyone: it had no reliable way to verify nationality in real time. On 30 June 2026 Anthropic announced that the export controls had been lifted. Access to Mythos 5 had already been restored for a set of United States organizations after government approval on 26 June. Fable 5 was to return to users worldwide on Anthropic's own platforms on 1 July, with access through AWS, Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry to follow as quickly as possible. An update dated 1 July 2026 reports access to both models restored. Other Anthropic models were not affected.

Read as a supplier event, three lessons follow, none of them about this supplier in particular.

1. **The trigger was outside the contract.** No commercial term was breached and no price changed. A governance plan that lists only supplier insolvency, price increases and model retirement as exit triggers was incomplete.
2. **A narrow restriction became a total one because of a control the supplier lacked.** Ask what your supplier can verify about your users, and what it will do to every customer when it cannot.
3. **Restoration was uneven.** Different channels and different classes of customer came back on different dates. A fallback must cover the whole interruption on the channel you actually use, not the supplier's headline date.

Open weights change the exposure without removing it. A supplier cannot withdraw weights you already hold, but the license, export rules and the law of the place you run them still bind you, and nobody is obliged to patch them. In an interruption of that kind, the only fallback a firm can count on is one it validated beforehand: another model already tested on its cases, a manual process or an honest notice to its own customers.

## 6. The flip side: is it fair play?

**The question.** If frontier model suppliers are building products their customers built first, is it now fair play for firms, and for developing economies in particular, to use AI to reverse engineer other firms' products and services and so shorten their own development cycles? A firm that sets out to do this will not do it on a closed model. It will use an open model it runs itself, under no supplier's terms and in no supplier's view, and consult closed models only occasionally. The question is about that firm.

**Check the premise first.** The record in Section 2 shows a supplier entering a market its customer serves. It does not show the supplier took the customer's product apart, and Section 3 explains why that difference matters in law. Even where a supplier does compete with its customers, another party's conduct does not change what the law permits you or what a counterparty can enforce against you. The question contains three, and they have different answers: is it lawful, is it fair and is it wise? A fourth comes first, because it explains how the work would be done: why an open model, and why the closed checks?

### Why an open model, and why the closed checks

For this work a firm has four reasons to prefer an open-weight model that it runs itself, and the first is the one most readers will already suspect.

1. **No supplier's terms in the way.** The closed suppliers' terms quoted below bar customers from using their services to build products or models that compete with the supplier, and Section 2 shows one such supplier now selling into two of its customers' markets, so a firm cannot always be sure its project falls outside those terms. A permissive license such as [Apache 2.0](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0), under which gpt-oss is released, grants a broad, perpetual license to reproduce, modify and distribute and carries no comparable restriction; other open-weight licenses do, so the license still has to be read.
2. **No supplier watching.** A closed supplier sees what is sent to it and can recognize a pattern in it; Anthropic's account of distillation, in Section 3, is an account of exactly that. A model run inside the firm leaves no record outside it.
3. **No supplier switch.** Access cannot be withdrawn halfway through the project, as it was for two models in Section 5.
4. **The firm's own secrets stay home.** What it learns about the rival's product, and its own design that follows, never leave its environment.

The fourth reason is entirely legitimate, and so is the third. A firm doing lawful work has good grounds to prefer independence and confidentiality.

The same firm will probably still consult closed models from time to time, where it judges the strongest of them better on the hardest cases, and it may spread those checks across several suppliers so that no one of them sees enough to notice. That design deserves a plain answer, because it is where the first two reasons stop being about independence and start being about not being found out.

- **The model is not the act.** Every test in the table below turns on acquisition, input, terms, output and record. None turns on which model did the work. An open model makes lawful reverse engineering private; it does not make an unlawful acquisition lawful or copied expression original.
- **Spreading traffic to stay unseen is the pattern suppliers look for.** Anthropic's account alleges campaigns that used fraudulent accounts and proxy services, spread their traffic across accounts and in one case mixed it with unrelated customer requests to make detection harder. It says it detected one of them while it was still running. Each supplier's terms apply to every check made through it, however few.
- **Concealment is evidence.** A plan whose purpose is to avoid accusation is a written record of what the firm believed about its own conduct. TRIPS footnote 10 reaches third parties who acquire information knowing, or grossly negligent in failing to know, that dishonest practices were involved; United States federal law allows exemplary damages of up to twice the award where misappropriation is willful and malicious ([18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1836)). And the open model leaves its own traces: the firm's logs, its people and the product it ships.

The defensible version uses both kinds of model for what each is good at. Run the open model in-house for confidentiality, independence and a clean record of independent derivation. Use closed models openly, under their terms, for checks the firm would be content to disclose. A design that works only if nobody finds out has already failed the tests below.

### Is it lawful?

**Issue.** May a firm use an AI model, open or closed, to study another firm's product or service and build its own version?

**Rule.** Five bodies of rule meet here; each is stated for the jurisdiction and date given, and none for any jurisdiction not named.

1. *Trade secrets.* United States federal law treats theft, bribery, misrepresentation, breach or inducement of a breach of a duty to maintain secrecy and espionage as improper means, and excludes reverse engineering and independent derivation ([18 U.S.C. § 1839(6)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-142/t18/s1839/)). In the European Union, [Article 3(1)(b) of Directive (EU) 2016/943](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016L0943) treats as lawful the observation, study, disassembly or testing of a product made available to the public or lawfully possessed, by someone free of any legally valid duty to limit acquisition of the trade secret. The international floor, [Article 39(2) of the TRIPS Agreement](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04d_e.htm), protects undisclosed information only against its disclosure, acquisition or use without consent "in a manner contrary to honest commercial practices", which footnote 10 says means at least breach of contract, breach of confidence and inducement to breach, and includes acquisition by third parties who knew, or were grossly negligent in failing to know, that such practices were involved.
2. *The target's terms.* A product bought or accessed under a license or terms of service may come with a promise not to reverse engineer it. Whether that promise is enforceable depends on the governing law.
3. *The model supplier's terms.* Anthropic's [Commercial Terms of Service](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms) (effective 17 June 2025), section D.4, bar customers from accessing the services to build a competing product or service, including to train competing AI models, and from reverse engineering or duplicating the services. OpenAI's [Services Agreement](https://openai.com/policies/services-agreement/) (effective 1 January 2026), section 3.3, bars reverse engineering the services and, except for permitted exceptions, using output to develop models that compete with OpenAI's products and services. Read in context, both protect the supplier against competition with itself; neither expressly addresses studying a third party's product.
4. *Copyright and patents.* Lawful reverse engineering yields understanding, not a license. It does not permit copying protected code, text or design, and it is no answer to a patent on what was understood: infringement under [35 U.S.C. § 271(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/35/271) turns on making, using, offering to sell or selling the patented invention in the United States, or importing it, without authority, however its workings were learned.
5. *Reach.* The market a firm sells into can act on conduct abroad. In [*TianRui Group Co. v. International Trade Commission*](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/581683), 661 F.3d 1322 (Fed. Cir. 2011), the court concluded that the Commission may grant relief based in part on conduct abroad, there in China, where necessary to protect a domestic industry from unfair competition, and affirmed the Commission's limited exclusion order against imported cast steel railway wheels.

**Application.** Studying a lawfully acquired product with a model's help is, in trade-secret law, the same act as studying it with an engineer: generally lawful where the acquisition was lawful and no binding promise forbids it. Four facts change the answer. *How the product was reached*: fraudulent accounts, borrowed credentials or a false identity is misrepresentation, one of the improper means the statute lists. *What went into the model*: material held under a confidentiality agreement does not become free to share because the recipient is software. *Whose product it is*: when the target is the model supplier's own service, its terms forbid the use, as Section 3's distillation allegations illustrate. *What came out*: an independently written specification is the reverse engineer's own; copied expression is not. Which model did the work is not among these facts.

**Conclusion.** "Fair play" is not a legal category. Reverse engineering a lawfully obtained product is a long-standing practice, lawful under United States federal trade-secret law and the European Union directive, and consistent with the TRIPS floor. A supplier's entry into its customers' markets gives no one a license to misrepresent access, breach a contract or copy protected expression, and the markets a developing firm most wants to reach are the ones best placed to enforce against it there. ⚠ Elsewhere, and on any fact beyond these, the answer depends on local trade-secret, unfair-competition and contract law; a firm planning this should take advice in each market it will sell into.

### Is it fair?

The historical case for the question is strong and should be put at full strength. Before 1891 foreign works had no copyright protection in the United States, and piracy of foreign works there, and of American works abroad, was rampant ([*Copyright Lore*](https://www.copyright.gov/history/lore/pdfs/200508%20CLore_August2005.pdf)); the International Copyright Act of that year was the first United States copyright law to authorize copyright relations with foreign countries ([United States Copyright Office](https://copyright.gov/timeline/timeline_19th_century.html)). Ha-Joon Chang's [*Kicking Away the Ladder*](https://anthempress.com/books/kicking-away-the-ladder-pb) (Anthem Press, 2002) argues that today's rich countries did not follow, while they were catching up, the policies and institutions they now press on others, and, in one reviewer's summary of the evidence, stole industrial technologies from one another. Korea's own first radio, in Section 3, began from a Japanese reference design.

The strongest answer to it is that the line history drew was never the nationality of the learner. It was the act of acquisition. GoldStar worked from a Sanyo model and learned from it; footnote 10 of TRIPS condemns breach and inducement, not study. That floor binds every member of the World Trade Organization ([Marrakesh Agreement, Article II:2](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/04-wto_e.htm)). And the standard of Section 3 runs in both directions: if a supplier's lawful entry into its customers' markets is competition, so is a Vietnamese or Korean firm's lawful reverse engineering of an American product; if obtaining access through fraudulent accounts is wrong when alleged against a Chinese laboratory, it is wrong whoever does it.

**The bad man's view.** There is a harder way to put the question. In an address at Boston University School of Law on 8 January 1897, Oliver Wendell Holmes told his audience that "if you want to know the law and nothing else, you must look at it as a bad man", one who cares only for the material consequences he can predict, and that the law is "the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious" ([*The Path of the Law*](https://cyber.harvard.edu/eon/ei/elabs/majesty/path.html), 10 Harv. L. Rev. 457). On that view "fair" drops out. What remains is a prediction: who can enforce, where and at what cost.

The question gains force when the referees weaken. At Davos on 20 January 2026, Canada's Prime Minister, Mark Carney, told the World Economic Forum that "we are in the midst of a rupture, not a transition". He argued that countries the rules no longer protect must protect themselves, and urged middle powers to act together so that others do not decide their fate for them ([transcript](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/01/davos-2026-special-address-by-mark-carney-prime-minister-of-canada)). The trading system's appellate referee is one measure of his point. On 25 September 2026 Colombia, for 130 members, put forward for the 100th time the proposal to start filling the vacancies on the World Trade Organization's Appellate Body, and for the 100th time the members could not launch the selection ([WTO](https://www.wto.org/english/news_e/news26_e/dsb_25sep26_496_e.htm)). His diagnosis is contested. At the same meeting the Dispute Settlement Body established a new panel, so first-instance adjudication continues, and three days after his speech the President of the European Central Bank, Christine Lagarde, said she was "not exactly on the same page as Mark" and would talk about alternatives rather than rupture ([RTÉ, reporting Reuters](https://www.rte.ie/news/business/2026/0123/1554678-world-order-shifting-but-it-is-not-a-rupture-lagarde/)). The bad man's lens does not depend on settling that argument. It only asks what will happen.

| Arena | The rules-based reading | The bad man's reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Great powers | Treaties, alliances and trade rulings bind. | Rules hold where the cost of breaking them exceeds the gain; tariffs, export controls and financial pressure are instruments. |
| Middle powers | Multilateral institutions supply security and market access. | Strategic autonomy and coalitions built issue by issue, so as to sit at the table rather than be bargained over. |
| International business | Supply chains are optimized for efficiency under uniform rules. | Legal and regulatory boundaries are priced as risks: localization, regulatory arbitrage, sanctions and tariffs in the cost base. |
| A firm using AI to learn from a rival | Reverse engineering is lawful or it is not. | Who can detect it, who can stop the product at the border and who can switch off the model? |

Run the bad man's calculation honestly for a firm in a developing economy and it does not come out where the question hopes. What has weakened is the multilateral referee, a forum in which smaller members could bring cases against larger ones. What has not weakened is enforcement by the stronger parties, in the places a firm needs to be. The United States, as an importing market, can exclude goods made with misappropriated secrets on the strength of conduct abroad (*TianRui*, above), and its courts can add exemplary damages of up to twice the award for willful and malicious misappropriation (18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(C)). The model supplier can detect a campaign and close it (Section 3). A government directive took two models away from every customer overnight (Section 5). A rupture removes the appeal; it does not remove the penalty. The bad man predicts more unilateral enforcement, not less, and he weighs the chance of being found out, which Section 3 shows is higher than the concealer assumes.

Holmes himself warned that he should not be read as speaking "the language of cynicism", since the law is "the witness and external deposit of our moral life" (*The Path of the Law*). The bad man is a method for knowing the law, not a model for conduct. Followed through, the method and the morality reach the same advice, which is also Carney's advice to middle powers translated to the firm: build leverage of your own rather than borrowing advantage that a stronger party can withdraw. For a firm working with AI, that means capability it has learned, an open model it runs itself, evaluation cases it owns and customers who would follow it to another supplier. Those survive a rupture. A copy does not, whichever model made it.

### Is it wise?

What a model reproduces quickly is the artifact. What Hobday's firms built over 20 to 30 years was the capability: their own designs, brands and distribution. A firm that uses AI to copy a product, rather than to learn, takes on three exposures at once, and running the work on an open model removes only part of the first.

1. **A residual dependency.** The open model frees the bulk of the work from any supplier. The closed checks do not: each runs under terms that may forbid competing with the supplier, in front of a supplier that can observe the use (Section 2) and whose access can end for reasons neither party controls (Section 5).
2. **A copy without the customer.** It owns a replica of the incumbent's product, not the incumbent's answer to the sentence in Section 1: the round trip, the installed base, the signature that stands behind the result.
3. **Exposure where the revenue is.** The evidence of how the product was made travels with it into every market where it is sold.

The use that compounds is learning: having the model read, explain and test what the firm can lawfully see, building the firm's own evaluation cases and keeping a dated record of independent derivation.

**Five tests before turning a model on someone else's product.**

| Test | The question to answer in writing |
| --- | --- |
| Acquisition | Did we buy, license or find this in public, or reach it through an account or terms that are not ours? |
| Input | Is everything we give the model ours to share with this supplier under our agreements and the law? |
| Terms | Which terms bind us: the target's, the model supplier's and our customers'? Does any forbid this use? |
| Output | Is what we will ship a specification we wrote, or does it contain someone else's code, text or design? |
| Record | Could we show a court or a customer in the market we sell into, from dated records, how we derived it? |


## 7. Five routes for one workflow

| Route | When it earns its place | What to establish before committing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep the existing process or use ordinary software | Rules are stable, the answer is exact or review consumes the savings. | Measure the current cost, time and error rate. Give this baseline the same acceptance test as any AI route. |
| Buy a closed model service | Performance on hard cases, faster entry or uncertain demand justifies buying capability by use. | Test on your cases. Check the agreement for data use, retention, change notice, indemnity and exit. Price revalidation after each model change. |
| Buy hosting for open weights | You want managed operations now and the ability to run the same model elsewhere later. | Obtain the licensed weights and configuration. Restore the workflow on a second host and compare outputs, latency and cost. Identify any feature only the first host supplies. |
| Operate open weights yourself or in the customer's environment | Local processing wins customers, a fixed version matters or sustained demand supports an operating team. | Test installation, upgrades, rollback, peak capacity and staffing. Trace where logs, backups and support data go. Include idle capacity in the price. |
| Use different models for different tasks | A dependable model handles routine work while a stronger one earns its premium on exceptions. | Define the routing rule, prohibited destinations and stop condition. Test misrouted cases. Budget for separate evaluations and incidents on each path. |

Distribution cuts across every row. You can keep your own interface, sell through the supplier's marketplace, expose a connector or deliver a signed professional work product inside the customer's existing process. For a software company an additional channel may justify a revenue share. For an advisory firm the customer may never see the model and still have reason to return: the adviser knows the business, stands behind the recommendation and helps implement it. Ask customers which part they would buy separately. Their answer may be less flattering, and more valuable, than the product roadmap.

## 8. What an accepted result costs, and what it earns

Tokens are an input. The saleable unit is an approved prototype, a correctly matched invoice or a contract review a lawyer can sign. Compare routes on the cost of producing the same accepted unit at the quality and speed the customer requires. [*Systems Architecture for AI*](systems-architecture-for-ai.md), Section 5, sets out the method: total cost of running and reviewing the work, failed attempts included, divided by results that pass acceptance, with referrals and critical errors reported separately.

**A break-even that review can erase.** A deliberately simplified example, fictional figures. A hosted service costs US$0.80 per accepted item. Operating open weights would add US$6,000 a month in fixed costs and US$0.20 per accepted item in variable costs. Assume equal quality, review effort and turnaround, enough capacity at peak and no other differences. The routes cost the same at **10,000 accepted items a month**: below that the hosted service is cheaper (US$4,000 against US$7,000 at 5,000 items), above it the self-operated route is (US$10,000 against US$16,000 at 20,000). Now suppose the self-operated route needs two more minutes of review per item at US$60 an hour. Its variable cost becomes US$2.20 per accepted item, above the hosted price, and no volume restores the break-even. Time the review on actual work before anyone prices a server.

**Revenue belongs in the same model.** A buyer that requires local processing may pay for an on-premises service below the break-even volume. A supplier feature included in a subscription the customer already holds may cut what that customer will pay you, even as your inference bill falls. Model the contribution per customer at renewal under both conditions, and ask which assumption the next pilot can resolve.

## 9. A decision a firm could actually make

An advisory firm sells contract portfolio reviews. Its Client wants a defensible list of renewal obligations, each tied to the controlling clause and amendment, with exceptions referred to counsel. The firm knows the Client's approval authorities and has the Client's permission to use its contract playbook for this engagement. A rival can buy the same model tomorrow. What the Client may still buy is the firm's ability to identify which amendment governs, resolve exceptions with the right person and stand behind the dates entered into the Client's system.

**Test.** Start with the renewal calendar the Client already keeps. Assemble a Client-authorized evaluation set of amendments, evergreen clauses, conflicting dates and contracts silent on renewal. Have counsel fix the expected disposition before seeing any model output. Compare the current process, a closed service where the Client permits it and an open-weight candidate inside the approved environment, with the reviewer blind to the route. Measure missed obligations, unsupported dates, reviewer minutes and cost per accepted record. Include a case whose correct answer is to stop and ask counsel.

**Decide.** Suppose the closed service handles ambiguous amendments best, some Client files must stay local and the firm has no staff to run its own serving infrastructure. A reasonable first decision is the closed service on authorized matters, with manual review for restricted files. A local route becomes attractive when enough Clients will pay for it or a qualified host can meet the restriction; if the local model already passes the routine cases, use it there. If neither model beats the calendar process after review, keep the calendar. In every version the firm controls the playbook, the approved record and the person authorized to release it, subject to the Client's rights.

**Then the harder fact.** The supplier launches contract review inside a subscription the Client already pays for. A second model protects the firm against an outage; it does nothing for a Client who no longer needs the firm. Invite the Client to run the supplier's product on the same authorized cases, and find out whether the Client will pay for the difference that remains. That is a strategy test an architecture diagram cannot perform.

## 10. The exercise

**Setting.** One existing product or proposed service. Three people: the product owner, the finance lead and the person accountable for the customer outcome. Sixty minutes in all. The frame is the five GRCnext™ questions this account uses to test whether a firm's choices survive a disruption: what service is promised, within what limits, along which routes, under whose control and with what way out.

| Time | Step |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 5 min | Each person writes, alone, the sentence from In brief, point 1. Compare. If the three sentences differ, that difference is the first finding. |
| 5 to 35 min | Complete the five questions below, using the question bank in Section 4 for each route under consideration. |
| 35 to 45 min | Run the simulated event: the supplier launches the job directly, access ends tonight as in Section 5, or a rival ships a copy of your product built with AI as in Section 6. Decide what the firm does in the next 72 hours. |
| 45 to 60 min | Debrief against the Definition of Done. |

| Question | What the decision must record |
| --- | --- |
| **Services** | What result does the customer accept? Where does the work begin, who invoices for it and who handles a disputed result? Name the strongest direct substitute, the supplier's own product included. |
| **Tolerances** | The minimum acceptable quality, the maximum cost per accepted result and the conditions for mandatory human review. Any data, license or customer restriction that rules out a route. |
| **Pipes** | One authorized case traced from intake through model, tools, logs and final record. Who can access each copy, and which contractual permission covers each step. |
| **Switches** | Who can pause, change provider or revert to the existing process; what evidence triggers that decision; how work in flight is reconciled. |
| **Exits** | Within 72 hours of a simulated supplier launch or loss of access, one lawful option for continuing the promised service, shown to work. The capacity, cost and customer commitments that option can actually support. |

**Definition of Done.** The decision names the chosen route, the strongest alternative and one fact that would reverse the choice. It attaches a representative evaluation, a cost comparison and evidence that the fallback works within the stated limits. Where evidence is missing, it authorizes only the pilot needed to obtain it, with a budget, an owner and a decision date. The production decision goes to the person who can accept the residual exposure, by name.

## 11. Discussion questions

1. Write the sentence from In brief, point 1, for your own firm. Would your three largest customers agree with it?
2. Your supplier publishes a success story about your product. What does it now know about your customers that you would not have told a competitor?
3. A connector puts your service inside the supplier's application. Which number would tell you, within two quarters, whether it is a channel or a substitute?
4. You reject a model because of where it was made or how it is alleged to have been trained. Would you apply the same evidentiary standard to a supplier from your own country?
5. Which of your exit triggers would have fired between 12 June and 1 July 2026? If none, what was your plan?
6. Is your advantage in the model, the data, the workflow, the relationship or the signature? Which of those can a supplier sell directly?
7. A competitor rebuilds your product from its public interface, on an open model it runs itself with occasional closed checks. Which of the five tests in Section 6 would it fail, and in which market could you act?
8. Your own team proposes to do the same to a rival. Would you be content to have the record of how you did it read aloud by the rival's counsel?
9. The proposal runs the work on an open model in-house and spreads occasional checks across several closed suppliers. Which part of that plan would you put in the board minutes, and which part would you rather not?
10. Run the bad man's calculation for your own project. Who holds enforcement power over it, in which market, and what would they do if they found out?
