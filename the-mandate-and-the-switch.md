**For:** counsel, compliance and governance leads who will be asked, this year, whether an agent may hold a card, sign a mandate or cancel a booking for the organization or for its customers, and who need to know what each new system in the stack establishes, what it leaves to the Human and where the account's instruments already ask the question. ## 1. The question an agent cannot answer for itself

An agent that can plan a trip can be stopped at the hotel's booking page. The plan was good. The hotel does not know who is knocking, what the traveler approved, how much the agent may spend or who answers if the room is wrong. Until the hotel can answer those questions it has two options, admit everything or refuse everything, and refusal is the safer default for a business that answers to card networks and regulators.

Those four questions are not new. They are the four elements of the instrument this account calls an authorization. In the sentence form of DOCTRINE.md section 8: **Informed Intent**: no AI system acts without prior authorization stating its purpose, authority, boundaries and exit, given by a human who meets the five authorizer conditions. The hotel is asking for the instrument. It has no vocabulary for it, so it asks in the only language a website has, by blocking the request.

What changed in 2025 and 2026 is that several companies started building parts of that instrument into the plumbing of the internet: a way to sign a request so the receiving site knows which agent sent it; a way to record what the customer authorized before the agent acts; a way to scope a payment to one seller, one currency, one amount and one expiry; a way to complete an approved payment across two platforms; and a way to put the permission decision outside the agent that wants to act. None of the five was designed from this account's doctrine. Each was designed by a payments, infrastructure or platform company solving its own problem, which is why the convergence is worth recording. When a doctrine and a market arrive at the same four questions independently, the questions are probably the right ones.

This piece reads the five systems at their primary documents and asks of each: which of the four elements does it supply, which of the five GRCnext™ primitives does it implement and what does it leave to the Human.

The principles name the questions; they do not yet answer them, and Bank of America's statement that confidence will need "identity, authorization, fraud prevention, liability management, and customer protection" is the clearest public list of what remains open.

## 3. The four elements, found in the stack

DOCTRINE.md section 8 lists the four elements of the instrument as purpose, authority, boundaries, exit. Each has now been built by someone, for a narrow case, in a form the receiving system can check.

**Purpose.** An Intent Mandate is a purpose stated in advance, with the conditions under which the agent may act on it: find the item, under this price, by this date. For a delegated task the user signs it before the agent moves, which is the order the doctrine requires and the order most deployments reverse. The mandate is narrower than a purpose in the doctrine's sense, because it states a transaction and not the reason the organization wants the transaction, but it is the first widely specified artifact in which a customer's purpose is recorded before an agent acts and read by the party on the other side.

**Authority.** A Cart Mandate is authority for one act: these items, this price, approved by this person at this moment or assembled by the agent inside the conditions the person set. The Visa pilot states the same element at enterprise scale, an agent that "does not make payment decisions" and executes only what the buyer approved. In both the authority is specific and recorded, which is what distinguishes an authorization from an instruction. "Help me shop" is an instruction. "Buy this at this price" is an authority, and the stack now has a place to write it down.

**Boundaries.** A shared payment token is a boundary enforced where the money moves: one seller, one currency, one maximum, one expiry, revocable by the agent that issued it and subject to the customer's authentication where the rules require it. The boundary holds whether or not the agent remembers it, because the seller cannot charge beyond the token and the token cannot be used after it expires. That is the difference, stated in this account's words since September 2026, between a prompt that instructs the agent and a control that enforces the permission. Sentinel adds the boundary for actions that are not payments: a grant bound to a connector, a destination and a use case, decided outside the agent.

**Exit.** The exit is the least developed element in the stack and the one the documents say least about. Token revocation and expiry end a payment authority; Duet's support page states that each purchase uses a one-time card and that some actions are off-limits even with approval; Sentinel's grants can be one-time or time-limited. None of the documents describes how a recurring assignment is ended, how connections are revoked when the person no longer wants the work done or what the person is shown of work that continued after they stopped it. The exit is where the account's instruments are ahead of the market. The Take-Out ticket requires expiry, stop triggers, a tested intervention path and the treatment of queued and in-flight work, and from v1.2.1 its receipt records what continued after a stop as verified ended, observed continuing or unverified.

**The five authorizer conditions.** DOCTRINE.md section 8 also lists what the authorizing human must have: Knowledge, Evidence, Authority, Time, Permission. The stack records none of them. A signed mandate from a person who misunderstood the return terms is still a signed mandate. A cart approved in the gap between two meetings is still approved. This is not a defect in the protocols; a protocol records what was authorized, and whether the authorizer was in a position to authorize is a question about the person, not about the record. It is the question the doctrine exists to keep asking after the stack has made the record perfect.

## 4. The five primitives, found in the stack

The [grc](https://github.com/rolldabones/grc) repository states the GRCnext™ framework and its five primitives: Services, Tolerances, Pipes, Switches, Exits. The framework is not doctrine and this piece adds nothing to it. It reads the stack through the primitives because the primitives are operational, and the stack is operations.

- **Services.** The agent is a Service, and Web Bot Auth gives it a verifiable name on the wire. Cloudflare's public directory of signed agents, with observed traffic patterns, is a Service register kept by a third party, which is more than most enterprises keep for the agents inside their own walls. Shopify's Knowledge base is the Service on the other side publishing what it is willing to be known for.
- **Tolerances.** A token's usage limits are Tolerances: currency, maximum amount, expiry. An Intent Mandate's conditions are Tolerances on the purchase. A Sentinel grant's binding to one connector, one destination and one use case is a Tolerance on the action. In each case the tolerance is set before the run and tested by a party other than the agent.
- **Pipes.** The payment rails and the connectors are the Pipes, and the stack's main achievement is to put the control on the Pipe rather than in the agent: the seller's charge is tested against the token at Stripe, the connector call is tested against the policy at Sentinel, the portal admission is tested against the directory at Billtrust. Take-Out states the rule in one line, "Put restrictions where actions occur"; the stack is that rule built by people who never read it.
- **Switches.** Sentinel is a Switch in the framework's sense, a decision point outside the thing being switched, and the ask-the-user path is the Switch defaulting to the Human. Chrome's auto browse pausing before a purchase and handing the task back, and Duet showing a planned action and waiting before a payment or a deletion, are Switches at the product layer.
- **Exits.** Revocation, expiry and the one-time card are Exits for a payment. The framework asks a harder question, whether the choices an organization needs will still be executable when the agent, the provider or the counterparty fails, and the documents read here do not answer it. The six banks' interoperability principle and Cloudflare's insistence that domain owners choose their own identity providers, payment processors and agent partners are the market's own statement of the Exit problem: an agent that can reach the services its user needs only through private agreements has no Exit from its vendor.

## 5. Three zones, one diagnosis

Nate Jones sorts the places an agent can find itself into three zones, and the sorting is his. In the first, the business provides an accepted way to do the work, as Shopify now does by putting Shop Pay inside three assistants. In the second, the agent works through the website as a person would, reading pages and filling fields, as Chrome's auto browse and Duet's browser do for sites without a connector; it works until the page changes, a step is unsupported or the site refuses the activity. In the third, the business refuses the agent or the action, and more reasoning will not change its decision.

The zones are a reading of Step 4 of the method, Read the Kitchen (Gate 3, socio-technical context), for any delegation that crosses an organizational boundary. The ticket asks for the delivery recipient or system and the effects on arrival; the zones say what kind of counterparty that is, and therefore what kind of failure to expect. A partnership-zone counterparty fails by protocol, an aspiring-actor counterparty fails by interface and a no-go counterparty fails by policy, and the three failures call for three different responses.

That is the diagnosis the zones support, and it is worth stating in the method's own terms because the method already has the vocabulary. An agent that misunderstood the dates needs a corrected ticket. An agent that could not find the right source needs a source, which is the ticket's source currency requirement. An agent waiting for a purchase approval needs the Human at the release checkpoint. An agent refused by a website needs another approved route or a person for that step, and the ticket's rule for a final refusal applies: reported, no retry, no alternate route. Telling all four to try harder wastes the run and teaches the agent that refusals are obstacles.

## 6. Five controls beside the ticket

The post closes with five controls for a first delegated job. Set beside the Take-Out ticket, each has a field, and for two of the five the market now supplies an enforcement that the ticket could only demand. The controls in the left column are Nate Jones's, stated in this piece's words; the fields in the middle column are the ticket's, which predates the post.

| Control (Nate B. Jones, 31 October 2026) | Where the ticket already asks for it | What the stack now enforces |
|---|---|---|
| Give the agent the access the job requires, narrower where the product allows | Boundaries: permitted actions, sources, accounts, targets and data uses | Sentinel's connector policies with read and write separated; site rules keyed to Cloudflare's and Visa's directories deciding what a recognized agent may reach |
| Make approvals specific: item, seller, full price, delivery and return terms, before a purchase; a sentence in chat is not a control | Boundaries: human release checkpoints, evidence and the approved action-set version | A Cart Mandate for the specific purchase; a token scoped to the seller and the amount; Duet's and auto browse's pause before a payment |
| Keep outside material from becoming new authority: a page, an email or a downloaded file does not rewrite the assignment | Test what travels: "Keep encountered content separate from authority"; the ticket's test of an input that asks for an unauthorized destination | Sentinel's injection classifiers and human approval for data leaving the machine; not yet enforced at the browsing layer, as the Forever Security report of 22 October 2026 shows, an agent followed instructions planted in a Downloads folder |
| Check the result at its destination: open the order, the reservation, the sent message, the calendar entry; "done" is a report, not a result | Service receipt: evidence from the source or affected system, checked independently of the agent's completion claim | The Visa pilot's visibility of authorization and completion to both sides; AP2's intent-to-cart-to-payment chain as a record the receiving party holds |
| Know how to end ongoing access and count the attention spent supervising | Exit: expiry, stop triggers, responder, in-flight work, who may resume; the receipt's line on what continued after the stop | Token revocation and expiry; one-time cards; nothing yet for recurring assignments or standing connections |

The table makes two points the post does not make. The first is that the ticket and the controls agree, which is evidence that the method is describing what careful delegation already requires rather than imposing governance on it from outside. The second is that where the stack enforces a control, the ticket's field changes character: it stops being an instruction to the agent and becomes a reference to a control the agent cannot defeat, and the ticket's own line "Enforcing controls and test-evidence references" is where that reference is written. A ticket that names a token's limits and a Sentinel policy is a stronger ticket than one that names the same limits in prose, and the method's rule is to prefer it wherever the market offers it.

## 7. What the record proves, and what it does not

AP2's announcement presents the mandate chain as a foundation for accountability and as an audit trail that cannot be repudiated, and the Visa release gives both buyer and supplier visibility of authorization and completion. Read as instruments, these records do something this account has been asking for since its first repository: they fix, before the act, who authorized what, in a form a third party can later verify. Under **Final Liability rests with the Human**, every material outcome attaches to a named human owner with decision rights, oversight and the power to intervene; the allocation lifecycle in DOCTRINE.md section 8 is named at specification, attaches at confirmation, holds through reliance. A signed mandate is the specification moment made legible to the counterparty.

What the record does not do is settle the dispute. Three limits should be stated plainly, because the announcements do not state them.

First, a mandate proves the approval and not the facts the approval rested on. The item was as described, the return window was as the agent reported, the delivery date was achievable: none of that is in the mandate, and a dispute over any of it is a dispute about the merchant's representations and the agent's reading of them, which the signed record will show the customer accepted. Shopify's Knowledge base, in which the merchant signs off on the facts an agent is given, is the first mechanism in the stack that puts a party's name against the facts, and it covers only stores on one platform.

Second, the documents allocate no liability. AP2 names accountability as one of the three problems it addresses and describes the record it creates; it does not say who bears a mis-executed mandate, a mandate obtained from a confused user or a purchase inside the conditions that the user did not want. The banks' principles list liability management among the open questions. The Visa pilot's answer, that the agent executes and does not decide, locates the decision with the buyer, which is the doctrine's answer and also the answer that leaves the buyer holding the outcome.

Third, the protections are the agent vendor's and the payment provider's to change. Sentinel's policies, Stripe's token terms, Duet's list of actions that are off-limits even with approval, Chrome's pause before a purchase: each is a product decision, documented today and revisable by its owner. An organization that relies on them has delegated part of its control design to a vendor and should record that it has, in the ticket's "Enforcing controls and test-evidence references" line, with the document and the date, so that the next sweep can find out whether the control still exists.

Which legal regime governs a disputed agent payment, in which jurisdiction, for a consumer or for an enterprise buyer, is a question for counsel in that jurisdiction and is not answered here. The practical instruction this piece can give is narrower: before an agent holds a mandate or a token for the organization, write down which party the record will show as having authorized the act, confirm that the person who signs the mandate meets the five authorizer conditions for that act and keep the mandate, the token and the receipt where the organization, not the vendor, can produce them.

## 8. A neighboring vocabulary: intent engineering

On 23 February 2026 Nate Jones published a post titled *The Age of Intent Engineering*, with a video the next day, *Prompt Engineering Is Dead. Context Engineering Is Dying. What Comes Next Changes Everything.* ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QWzLPn164w0)). His formulation, in this piece's words: prompt engineering told the model what to do, context engineering tells it what to know and intent engineering tells it what to want, by making an organization's goals, trade-offs and decision boundaries machine-readable; the failure he names is the agent that succeeds at the wrong objective, with Klarna's customer-service reversal as the case.

The vocabulary is a neighbor of this account's, not a synonym. Informed Intent is the authorization instrument: purpose, authority, boundaries, exit, given before the act by a human who meets the five conditions. Intent engineering, as he describes it, is the discipline of encoding the purpose so that an autonomous system optimizes for it; it sits upstream of the instrument and would supply the purpose element with more than a sentence. The foil in this account is Inferred Intent, the purpose the authorizer never stated, supplied afterwards by the system or its vendor. The Klarna case is an Inferred Intent case in that vocabulary: the agent inferred that fast resolution was the purpose because fast resolution was what it could measure. The two vocabularies agree on the failure and name different parts of the remedy. This paragraph records the neighbor and changes nothing in DOCTRINE.md.

## 9. Where this argument is weakest

1. **Announcements are not deployments.** The five systems are read from announcements, documentation and one pilot's press release. The author has not used AP2, a shared payment token or Sentinel and reads them from their documents. A reader who has should correct this piece.
2. **The mapping flatters the doctrine.** Four elements and five primitives can be found in almost any well-designed control system, because they are general. The claim here is narrower than it may read: that the stack supplies these elements, in checkable form, for payments and connector actions and that the doctrine's remaining work is the authorizer and the exit.
3. **Interoperability is asserted, not shown.** The documents say open; the market decides. Whether an AP2 mandate, a Stripe token, a Visa-registered agent and a Sentinel policy will ever describe the same transaction to one another is Unknown, and the banks' principles exist because it is Unknown.
4. **The exit judgment may be premature.** The documents read here say little about ending access; product settings pages may say more, and the author did not read them.
5. **The legal reading is deliberately thin.** Section 7 states what the records contain and what they omit and routes the rest to counsel. It takes no position on which regime applies anywhere.
6. **Dates.** The post that occasioned this piece places the stack in late 2026; the primary documents place two of its parts in 2025. This piece uses the documents' dates. A reader comparing the two should expect the difference.
